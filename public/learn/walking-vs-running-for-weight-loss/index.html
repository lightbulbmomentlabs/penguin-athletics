Walking vs running for weight loss: which works better?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 26, 2026 6 min read

The short answer

Running usually has the edge for weight loss, mostly because it uses about twice as much energy per minute as brisk walking. In a study of about 47,000 walkers and runners, the same amount of exercise energy was linked to more weight loss from running, but walkers were better at keeping their habit going. The best choice is the one you'll do most weeks, ideally alongside changes in eating.

If your goal is weight, it's tempting to assume running wins by a mile. It's harder, it's sweatier, and it feels like it should count for more.

The research mostly agrees, but with a twist. Running does use more energy, and one big study linked it to more weight loss. Walking, though, has a superpower that matters just as much: people tend to keep doing it.

Here's what the studies show on energy use, weight change, appetite, and injuries, plus how to choose. (For the full picture, see our guide to walking for weight loss.)

Does running burn more calories than walking?

Per minute, yes, by a lot. The CDC's chart for a 154-pound person lists about 140 calories for 30 minutes of walking at 3.5 mph and about 295 calories for 30 minutes of running at 5 mph. That's roughly double. Brisk walking at 4.5 mph sits in between, at about 230 calories.

The 2024 Compendium of Physical Activities, the main reference researchers use, tells the same story in METs (multiples of your resting energy use). Brisk walking at 3.5 to 3.9 mph is rated at 4.8 METs. General jogging is 7.5 METs, and running at 6 to 6.3 mph (a 10-minute mile) is 9.3 METs.

All of these are estimates. The CDC notes that people who weigh more than 154 pounds use more energy and people who weigh less use less. For more on how these numbers work, see how many calories does walking burn?

What about per mile?

The gap shrinks when you compare the same distance, because walking takes longer. One lab study measured 15 women and 15 men in their early 20s as they walked and ran 1,600 meters, about a mile. The walk was at about 3.2 mph and the run at about 6 mph.

  • During the mile, the walk used 372.54 kilojoules (about 89 calories) and the run used 471.03 kilojoules (about 113 calories).
  • Counting the 30 minutes after, the walk totaled 463.34 kilojoules (about 111 calories) and the run 664.00 kilojoules (about 159 calories).

So running a mile used more energy than walking it, partly because your body keeps working harder for longer after a run. The study's authors concluded that both are good ways to raise energy use above rest.

Is running better than walking for weight loss?

The best head-to-head evidence comes from a study of 15,237 walkers and 32,216 runners who filled out surveys about their exercise and weight, then again 6.2 years later.

More exercise was linked to less weight gain for both groups. But for the same amount of exercise energy, running was linked to more weight loss than walking. In men, that held across every weight group. In women, it showed up in the heaviest quarter of the group. In that heaviest quarter, weight loss per unit of exercise energy was 90% greater for running than walking, in both women and men.

Two other findings are worth knowing:

  • Running was linked to less weight gain with age in both women and men. Walking was too, but only in women.
  • Walkers kept it up better. Over the 6 years, walkers' exercise dropped off much less than runners' did.

This was an observational study, and people reported their own exercise and weight. The author noted it can't show whether exercise changed first or weight did, and it lacked detailed diet data. So it shows a strong link, not proof that running caused the difference.

Does exercise intensity really matter for fat?

Less than you might think, at least in the short term. A review of 13 trials in adults aged 18 to 45 with higher body weight compared hard interval training with steady, moderate exercise. Both reduced total body fat and waist size, with no significant difference between them. The interval training just took about 40% less time.

The authors noted these body fat changes came without matching changes on the scale. The effects showed up with running-based training but not cycling, and the review didn't test walking.

Walking trials show a smaller but real effect. A review of 32 trials in inactive adults found walking alone lowered weight by about 1.37 kg, waist size by about 1.5 cm, and body fat by about 1.2 percentage points. More on that in can you lose weight just by walking?

Does running make you hungrier than walking?

Probably not, based on early evidence. In one small study, 9 women who ran and 10 women who walked each did 60 minutes of exercise at the same relative effort, then ate a meal they chose themselves. The women were in their mid-20s.

After subtracting the energy each workout used, the runners' meal came out about 194 calories below what they'd used, while the walkers' came out about 41 calories above. Both groups ended up lower than after resting. Running changed several appetite hormones, while walking didn't.

That's one small study with different people in each group, so treat it as a clue, not a rule. Appetite after exercise varies a lot from person to person.

Which is easier to stick with?

This is where walking pulls ahead, and it matters a lot for weight over the long run.

Running carries a higher injury risk, especially early on. A review found new runners had about 17.8 injuries per 1,000 hours of running, compared with 7.7 for more experienced recreational runners. The authors noted that studies defined injuries differently. An injury that keeps you on the couch for a month won't help your weight goals.

And remember: in the big survey study, walkers were more likely to keep up their exercise over the years. The most effective workout is the one you're still doing next spring.

How much do you need, either way?

The CDC's baseline for adults is 150 minutes a week of moderate activity like brisk walking, or 75 minutes a week of vigorous activity like running, or a mix. A brisk walk means about a 15-minute mile.

For weight specifically, the CDC says you'll need a high amount of activity to lose weight and keep it off, unless you also change how you eat. The exact amount varies greatly from person to person. For a step target, see how many steps a day to lose weight?

How to choose (and how to mix them)

  1. Start with walking if you're new to exercise. Aim for 30 minutes of brisk walking most days. A lunch walk or walking part of your commute counts.
  2. Add hills to close the gap. Walking up a 6% to 10% grade is rated at 7.0 METs, close to general jogging at 7.5. A hilly route or a treadmill incline works.
  3. Try short jog intervals if you're curious. Jog for a minute, walk for a few, and repeat. Interval-style training saved time in trials.
  4. Build running slowly. New runners get hurt more often. Add a little at a time and take rest days.
  5. Pick the one you like. Walkers were better at sticking with it, and consistency beats intensity.
  6. Pair either one with eating changes if weight is your goal. A doctor or registered dietitian can help you find an approach that fits your life.

When should you talk to your doctor?

Check with your doctor first, especially before you start running, if any of these apply:

  • You have heart disease, high blood pressure, or diabetes.
  • You take medicine that affects your heart rate, blood pressure, or blood sugar.
  • You're pregnant or recently gave birth. Ask your doctor or midwife what's right for you.
  • You have joint pain, a past injury, or a higher risk of falls.
  • You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.
  • You have a history of an eating disorder, or tracking calories affects your mental health.

FAQ

Is a brisk walk as good as a slow jog?

Not quite per minute. Brisk walking at 3.5 to 3.9 mph is rated at 4.8 METs, while general jogging is 7.5 METs. Walking longer or adding hills closes the gap.

Is incline walking as good as running?

It can come close. Walking up a 6% to 10% grade is rated at 7.0 METs, near general jogging at 7.5 METs.

Does running burn more belly fat?

There's no good evidence that running targets belly fat. In trials, steady and interval exercise both trimmed waist size about equally. See can walking reduce belly fat?

Can I lose weight walking instead of running?

Yes, but usually slowly. Walking-only trials found about 1.37 kg of weight loss on average. Adding eating changes helps more.

    Weight · 6 min readYes. Trials show that walking soon after you eat lowers the blood sugar rise that follows a meal. In a 2026 review of weight-loss trials, people who raised their steps to about 8,500 a day and stayed there kept more weight off, but the extra steps didn't speed up the initial loss. Walking on its own usually leads to modest weight loss, so steps work best alongside eating changes.
