Step counters made "10,000 steps" sound like the finish line. But when researchers actually track people's steps and follow them for years, the picture is more encouraging than that.

The short version: more steps are linked to a lower risk of dying early, the biggest gains come at the low end, and the benefit starts to level off well before 10,000 for many people. Here's what the research shows, and how to use it. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to [walking and longevity](/learn/walking-longevity/).)

## What do the biggest step studies show?

The strongest evidence comes from studies that pool data from many groups of people who wore step counters and were then followed for years.

A 2022 meta-analysis in *The Lancet Public Health* combined 15 studies and more than 47,000 adults, followed for a median of about 7 years [1]. People were split into four groups by daily steps. Compared with the lowest group (a median of about 3,550 steps a day), the risk of dying during the study was [1]:

- about **40% lower** for people around 5,800 steps a day
- about **45% lower** for people around 7,800 steps a day
- about **53% lower** for people around 10,900 steps a day

A 2025 review in the same journal pooled studies published over the past decade [2]. Compared with 2,000 steps a day, **7,000 steps a day was linked to a 47% lower risk of death from any cause** [2]. It was also linked to lower risks of heart disease, cancer death, type 2 diabetes, dementia, depressive symptoms, and falls [2].

A third meta-analysis, of 17 studies and about 227,000 people, found that each extra **1,000 steps a day was linked to a 15% lower risk** of death from any cause [3].

### What these studies can and can't tell you

These are observational studies. They show that people who walk more tend to live longer, not that walking alone is the reason. People who walk a lot may also differ in other ways, like overall health. Researchers adjust for many of these factors, but some may remain [2].

Still, many large studies from several countries point the same way [1][2]. That's why health researchers take them seriously.

## Where does the benefit level off?

The curve isn't a straight line. The drop in risk is steepest when people go from very few steps to a moderate number, then it flattens out.

The 2022 analysis found that the risk kept falling until about **6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for adults 60 and older**, and about **8,000 to 10,000 steps a day for adults younger than 60** [1]. Beyond those points, extra steps didn't seem to lower risk much further in that data.

Other studies land in a similar range:

- In about 16,700 women with an average age of 72, risk fell steadily until about **7,500 steps a day**, then leveled off [4].
- The 2025 review saw the curve for death from any cause start to bend at around **5,000 to 7,000 steps a day** [2].
- A UK study of 78,500 adults aged 40 to 79 linked more steps to lower risk of death up to about **10,000 steps a day** [5].

Not every study shows a clear ceiling. The 17-study review looked at counts as high as 20,000 steps a day and described the pattern as "the more the better" [3]. So if you already walk a lot, there's no sign that more is harmful.

### Is it different if you're 60 or older?

It seems to be. In the 2022 analysis, older adults reached the low-risk zone at a lower step count than younger adults: about 6,000 to 8,000 a day, compared with 8,000 to 10,000 [1].

The study of older women points the same way. Women who took about 4,400 steps a day had a lower death rate than women who took about 2,700 [4]. That's a modest daily count with a meaningful link to longer life.

## Where did 10,000 steps come from?

Not from a study. Researchers who studied steps in older women noted that the origin of the 10,000 goal is unclear [4]. They wrote that it likely comes from the name of a pedometer sold in Japan in 1965, called *Manpo-kei*, which translates to "10,000 steps meter" [4].

The 2022 meta-analysis made a similar point: 10,000 steps is widely promoted, but there was little evidence behind it [1]. The 2025 review concluded that 10,000 can still be a good target for people who are already active, but 7,000 may be a more realistic goal for others [2].

So 10,000 isn't wrong. It's just not a magic threshold, and falling short of it doesn't mean your walking doesn't count.

## Does walking speed matter?

Probably less than total steps, though the evidence is mixed.

Researchers measure speed as *cadence*, or steps per minute. In a large U.S. study of adults 40 and older, faster stepping was not linked to lower risk of death once total daily steps were taken into account [6]. The study of older women found the same thing [4].

The 2022 meta-analysis found that people whose fastest 30 minutes of the day were quicker had a lower risk, even after accounting for total steps [1]. But time spent walking at 100 steps a minute or faster wasn't clearly linked to lower risk [1]. The UK study found that a faster peak 30-minute pace was linked to lower risk beyond total steps [5].

The takeaway: **getting your steps in matters most.** Adding some brisk walking may offer a little extra, and it's a good way to fit more steps into less time. But a slow step still counts.

## What if you're starting from a low step count?

This is where the news is best. The biggest drops in risk show up at the bottom of the range, so the people with the lowest counts have the most to gain.

In the large U.S. study, adults who took 8,000 steps a day had about **half the risk of death** of those who took 4,000 [6]. Moving from 4,000 to 8,000 is a big jump, but you don't have to make it all at once. The review linking each extra 1,000 steps to a 15% lower risk suggests that every step up the ladder may be worth it [3].

The CDC puts it simply: some physical activity is better than none [7]. If you're at 3,000 steps today, 4,000 is a real win. (If the whole idea of exercise makes you groan, try our tips on [how to start walking when you hate exercise](/learn/how-to-start-walking-when-you-hate-exercise/).)

## A practical step plan for a longer life

You don't need a perfect plan. You need one you'll keep doing. Try this:

1. **Find your baseline.** Check your phone or watch for your average daily steps over the past week. That's your starting point, not a grade.
2. **Add about 1,000 steps a day.** A short walk after a meal or a lap around the block can do it. Hold that for a week or two.
3. **Keep climbing in small jumps.** Add another 1,000 when the last one feels normal. Each step up is linked to lower risk [3].
4. **Aim for the range that fits your age.** Around 6,000 to 8,000 a day if you're 60 or older, and 8,000 to 10,000 if you're younger [1]. If you're already past that, keep going.
5. **Make some of it brisk.** The CDC recommends 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, such as brisk walking [7]. Thirty minutes, five days a week, gets you there [7].
6. **Add strength and balance.** Adults 65 and older are advised to do muscle-strengthening activities at least 2 days a week, plus activities that improve balance [8].
7. **Watch your weekly average, not one day.** A low-step day happens. What matters is the pattern over weeks and months.

## When should you talk to your doctor?

Walking is safe for most people. But check with your doctor before ramping up if any of these apply:

- You have heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, or another long-term condition.
- You've had a fall recently, or you feel unsteady on your feet.
- You have joint pain or an injury that walking makes worse.
- You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.

For older adults and people with health conditions, the CDC advises being as active as your abilities and conditions allow [8]. Your doctor can help you figure out a safe starting point. ## FAQ

### Is 7,000 steps a day enough?

For many people, it's a strong target. A 2025 review linked 7,000 steps a day to a 47% lower risk of death than 2,000 steps [2].

### Do more than 10,000 steps help?

Some studies see little extra benefit past about 10,000 steps a day [1][5]. One review covering up to 20,000 steps found risk kept falling [3], so more doesn't appear to hurt.

### Do steps from daily chores count?

Studies that measured steps with a device counted slower, everyday steps too, not just walks taken on purpose [4][5]. So yes, steps around the house and at work are part of the total.

### Should older adults aim lower?

The lowest risk for adults 60 and older showed up at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day, lower than for younger adults [1].
