How many calories does walking burn?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 7, 2026 6 min read

The short answer

A 30-minute brisk walk uses roughly 140 to 240 calories for most adults, and in one study, walking a mile used about 90 to 110 calories. The biggest factors are your body weight, your pace, and whether you're going uphill. Every calorie number, including the one on your watch, is an estimate, so treat it as a rough guide.

It's one of the first things people want to know when they start walking: how much energy does this actually use? Your watch, your treadmill, and every app has a number. They rarely agree.

There's a reason for that. The energy you use walking depends on your body, your pace, and the ground under your feet, and no device measures it directly. Here's what researchers use to estimate it, what the numbers look like, and how much to trust them. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to walking for weight loss.)

How do scientists estimate calories from walking?

Researchers use a unit called a MET (metabolic equivalent). One MET is a standard estimate of the energy your body uses at rest, about 1 kcal per kilogram of body weight per hour [1]. An activity rated at 4 METs uses about four times your resting energy.

The main reference for these ratings is the Compendium of Physical Activities. Its 2024 update pulled energy costs from 701 published papers and covers 1,114 activities for adults aged 19 to 59 [1].

That gives a simple rule of thumb for estimating calories:

METs × your weight in kilograms × hours of walking ≈ calories used

The "1 kcal per kilogram per hour" shortcut is a scientific convention, not a measurement of you [1][3]. We'll get to how far off it can be.

How much energy does each walking pace use?

The 2024 Compendium rates walking on a firm, level surface like this [2]:

  • 2.5 mph (an easy stroll): 3.0 METs
  • 2.8 to 3.4 mph (moderate pace): 3.8 METs
  • 3.5 to 3.9 mph (brisk, walking for exercise): 4.8 METs
  • 4.0 to 4.4 mph (very brisk): 5.5 METs

The CDC counts brisk walking as moderate-intensity activity and gives "a 15-minute mile" as an example [8]. That's 4 mph.

So what does 30 minutes look like?

Using the rule of thumb, here are rough estimates for a person who weighs 70 kg (154 pounds), walking for 30 minutes [1][2]:

  • Easy stroll (2.5 mph): about 105 calories
  • Moderate pace (2.8 to 3.4 mph): about 133 calories
  • Brisk pace (3.5 to 3.9 mph): about 168 calories
  • Very brisk pace (4.0 to 4.4 mph): about 193 calories

The CDC's own chart, adapted from older federal guidelines, lands in the same neighborhood. It lists about 140 calories for 30 minutes of walking at 3.5 mph for a 154-pound person, and about 230 calories at 4.5 mph [8].

How much does body weight change the number?

A lot. METs are calculated per kilogram of body weight, so the same walk uses more total energy for a person who weighs more [1]. The CDC notes that calories used "will be higher for persons who weigh more than 154 lbs (70 kg) and lower for people who weigh less" [8].

Here's the same 30-minute brisk walk (4.8 METs) for three body weights [1][2]:

  • 60 kg (132 lb): about 144 calories
  • 80 kg (176 lb): about 192 calories
  • 100 kg (220 lb): about 240 calories

One more detail: those totals include the energy you'd use anyway just by being alive. One of those METs is your resting energy [1]. So the extra energy from a 30-minute brisk walk, for a 70 kg person, is closer to 133 calories than 168.

How much do hills and incline add?

Hills make a big difference. On the Compendium's list, walking up a 1% to 5% grade at a moderate-to-brisk pace is rated at 5.3 METs [2]. On a 6% to 10% grade, it jumps to 7.0 METs [2]. That's more than double an easy stroll on flat ground.

For a 70 kg person, 30 minutes on that steeper grade works out to roughly 245 calories [1][2]. A hilly route or a treadmill incline is the easiest way to make the same amount of time count for more.

Carrying a load adds energy too. The Compendium rates walking with a 21 to 40 pound load up a 3% to 10% grade at 7.5 METs [2].

How many calories does walking a mile burn?

Distance is a handy way to think about it, because walking faster also means finishing sooner. One study measured 30 adults with average fitness as they walked and ran 1,600 meters, which is just about a mile [4]. They averaged 71 kg (about 157 pounds) [4].

  • The walk (about 3.2 mph) used 372.54 kilojoules, roughly 89 calories [4].
  • Including the 30 minutes after, when the body is still recovering, the walk totaled 463.34 kilojoules, roughly 111 calories [4].
  • The run used more: 664.00 kilojoules in total, roughly 159 calories [4].

So running the same distance used more energy, but walking still counted. The researchers concluded that both walking and running are good ways to raise energy use above rest [4].

Can you trust these numbers?

Treat them as ballpark figures. Here's why.

The "1 MET" shortcut doesn't fit everyone

The standard value for one MET was first based on the resting oxygen use of a single 70 kg, 40-year-old man [3]. When researchers measured 769 adults, their average resting energy use was lower than that standard [3]. The usual "1 kcal per kilogram per hour" figure overestimated resting energy by 20% on average [3]. Body makeup, meaning fat mass and lean mass, explained most of the difference between people [3].

The Compendium team acknowledges this. Its website explains that the 3.5 ml/kg/min resting value can overestimate the resting rate of people who actually use less, and it offers "corrected METs" as an adjustment [9].

Common formulas can also miss in the other direction

A 2016 analysis compared walking energy measured in 25 groups of people with what standard equations predicted [5]. Two widely used equations, including one from the American College of Sports Medicine, underpredicted nearly all 127 measured values [5]. An equation that included height, weight, and speed had errors nearly four times smaller [5].

In other words, estimates can be too high for some people and too low for others.

Your watch is guessing too

Wearables don't measure calories directly. A review of 60 studies found that wrist and arm-worn monitors "differ in accuracy depending on activity type" [6]. Devices that combined heart rate with motion sensors tended to do better [6].

A review of 67 Fitbit studies was blunter. Fitbit devices "were unlikely to provide accurate measures for energy expenditure in any testing condition" [7]. Step counts were much more reliable [7]. Our beginner's guide to walking covers tracker accuracy in more detail.

How to use calorie numbers without letting them run your life

Calorie estimates can be useful for curiosity and planning. They're a shaky foundation for strict math. Try this instead:

  1. Use calories as a rough guide, not a target. Every number above could be off by a meaningful amount for you [3][5][7].
  2. Track steps or minutes instead. Step counts are more reliable than calorie readouts [7], and minutes are easy to count. The CDC's baseline is at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity like brisk walking [8].
  3. Pick up the pace when you can. Moving from a stroll to a brisk walk raises the energy cost by more than half [2].
  4. Find a hill. Even a gentle grade adds noticeably more energy use than flat ground [2].
  5. Remember what calories don't capture. Walking helps your blood pressure, blood sugar, and fitness even when weight barely changes. Read more in can you lose weight just by walking?
  6. Don't treat walking as payback for food. The CDC notes that "most weight loss occurs from decreasing calories," not from activity alone [8]. Walk because it's good for you, not to cancel anything out.

When should you talk to your doctor?

Walking is safe for most people. Check with your doctor first if any of these apply:

  • You have heart disease, high blood pressure, or diabetes.
  • You take medicine that affects your heart rate, blood pressure, or blood sugar.
  • You have joint pain, an injury, or a higher risk of falls, especially before adding hills or carrying weight.
  • You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.
  • You have a history of an eating disorder, or counting calories affects your mental health. A doctor or registered dietitian can help you find goals that don't revolve around numbers.

FAQ

Does walking faster burn more calories per mile?

A little. Faster paces have higher MET ratings, but you also finish sooner, so the per-mile difference is smaller than the per-minute one [2]. Running the same mile used more energy than walking it in one study [4].

Is treadmill walking the same as outside?

The Compendium rates level treadmill walking about the same as walking on a firm, level surface at the same speed [2]. Adding incline raises the cost [2].

Why do apps show different calorie numbers?

They use different formulas and sensors. Wrist-worn devices vary in accuracy by activity type, and research shows calorie estimates are far less reliable than step counts [6][7].

Do heavier people burn more walking?

Yes. The same walk uses more total energy for a person with a higher body weight, because energy cost scales with weight [1][8].

  • Weight · 6 min read

    Can you lose weight just by walking?

    Yes, but usually only a little. In trials of walking alone, people lost about 1.4 kg (roughly 3 pounds) on average, and programs that combine walking with eating changes lead to more weight loss. Walking still improves blood pressure, blood sugar, and fitness even when the scale barely moves.
  • Basics · 7 min read

    How to start walking when you hate exercise

    Start smaller than you think you need to, like a 5 to 10 minute walk tied to something you already do every day, and add a few minutes each week. Health guidelines say some activity is better than none and short walks count. New habits took about 66 days on average to feel automatic in one study, so aim for consistency, not intensity.
  • Longevity · 7 min read

    How many steps a day to live longer?

    In a pooled analysis of 15 studies, the risk of dying early kept falling as steps rose, then leveled off at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for adults 60 and older and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults. You don't need 10,000 to benefit: a 2025 review linked 7,000 steps a day to a 47% lower risk of death than 2,000 steps.
  • Heart · 6 min read

    How many steps a day do you need for heart health?

    You probably don't need 10,000. In large studies, people who took more steps had a lower risk of heart disease, with most of the benefit showing up by about 7,000 to 9,000 steps a day. In adults 60 and older, 6,000 to 9,000 steps a day was linked to a 40% to 50% lower risk of heart disease than about 2,000 steps.
