A lot of people quietly assume walking doesn't "count." Real cardio, the thinking goes, means sweating through a spin class or dragging yourself around a track.

The research disagrees. Walking at a brisk pace is officially moderate-intensity exercise, and it has some of the best evidence of any activity for helping your heart.

The catch is the word *brisk*. A slow stroll to the coffee machine is good for you, but it's not the same workout. Here's what counts, how much you need, and how to check your pace. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to [walking and your heart](/learn/walking-heart-health/).)

## Does walking actually count as cardio?

Yes, if you walk briskly. The CDC lists "walking briskly (2.5 miles per hour or faster)" as a moderate-intensity aerobic activity [1]. That's the same category as other activities that get your heart rate up and your breathing a little harder.

Adults need at least **150 minutes a week** of moderate-intensity activity, such as 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week [2]. Brisk walking can make up all of it. You can also break it into smaller chunks across the week [2].

Going past 150 minutes brings even more health benefits [2]. And if 150 sounds like a lot right now, the CDC is clear that some activity is better than none [2].

## What does brisk walking do for your heart?

### It improves fitness and blood pressure

The strongest evidence comes from trials, where inactive adults were assigned to walk or not. A review of 32 walking trials found that walking raised aerobic capacity, a key measure of cardio fitness [3]. It also lowered systolic blood pressure (the top number) by about 3.6 points and diastolic (the bottom number) by about 1.5 points [3].

Walkers also saw small drops in waist size, weight, and body fat [3]. Walking didn't change cholesterol levels in these trials [3]. The authors concluded that walking "improve[s] many risk factors" for heart disease [3].

### It's linked to lower heart disease risk

Long-term studies point the same way. A meta-analysis of 18 studies with nearly 460,000 people found that those who walked the most had a 31% lower risk of cardiovascular disease than those who walked the least [4]. The results held for both men and women [4].

Another review found that about 30 minutes of walking a day, 5 days a week, was linked to a 19% lower risk of coronary heart disease [5]. The risk kept dropping as walking went up [5]. These are observational studies, so they show strong links, not proof.

## Is walking as good as harder workouts?

For heart risk, it can hold its own. A large study of 73,743 women in the Women's Health Initiative compared walking with vigorous exercise [6]. Walking and vigorous exercise "were associated with similar risk reductions" for heart and blood vessel events [6].

That study followed women aged 50 to 79 after menopause, so it isn't a perfect match if you're in your 20s or 30s [6]. But it's one of the biggest studies of walking in women, and the pattern is encouraging.

The real difference is time. The guidelines treat 75 minutes of vigorous activity, like running, as equal to 150 minutes of moderate activity [2]. So walking gets you to the same weekly goal. It just asks for more minutes. For the full head-to-head, see [walking vs running: which is better for your heart?](/learn/walking-vs-running-which-is-better-for-your-heart/)

## Does your pace matter?

It seems to. In the meta-analysis of 18 studies, walking pace was a stronger predictor of lower risk than how much people walked [4].

A pooled study of more than 50,000 walkers in Britain found something similar [7]. Compared with slow walkers, people who said they walked at an average pace had a 24% lower risk of dying from cardiovascular disease, and brisk or fast walkers had a 21% lower risk [7]. Results didn't differ between men and women [7]. People rated their own pace, so treat this as a link, not proof.

The authors suggested that picking up the pace could be a useful message when walking more often isn't realistic [7]. That's handy if your schedule is packed. If you want more on speed, see [walking speed and life expectancy](/learn/walking-speed-and-life-expectancy-what-s-the-link/).

## How do you know you're walking briskly?

You don't need a heart-rate monitor. Try one of these checks.

### The talk test

During moderate activity, you can talk, but not sing [1]. If you can belt out a whole chorus, speed up. If you can only get out a few words before stopping for breath, you've moved into vigorous territory [1].

### The effort scale

On a scale of 0 (sitting) to 10 (all-out), moderate activity feels like a 5 or 6 [1]. Vigorous starts at about 7 or 8 [1].

### Steps per minute

This one comes from research in your age group. A lab study of 76 adults aged 21 to 40, half women, measured how hard people were working at different walking speeds [8]. About **100 steps per minute** was a good marker of moderate intensity, and about 130 steps per minute marked vigorous [8]. The 100 steps-per-minute cutoff correctly sorted 87.6% of walking bouts [8], and adding sex to the model didn't change the threshold [8].

To check, count your steps for 15 seconds and multiply by 4. If you're at 25 or more in 15 seconds, you're likely in the brisk zone.

## How to make your walks count as cardio

1. **Pick up the pace for at least part of the walk.** Use the talk test: chatty, but not singing [1].
2. **Aim for 150 minutes a week.** That's 30 minutes, 5 days a week, or any mix that adds up [2].
3. **Split it up if you need to.** Two 15-minute walks, like a lunch loop and an after-work lap, count the same as one longer one [2].
4. **Count your cadence once in a while.** Around 100 steps per minute is a good target [8].
5. **Add strength on 2 days.** The guidelines also recommend muscle-strengthening activity at least 2 days a week [2]. Walking covers cardio, not that.
6. **Track the trend.** Look at your weekly total, not one slow day. See [how many steps a day you need for heart health](/learn/how-many-steps-a-day-do-you-need-for-heart-health/).

## What about during pregnancy?

The CDC recommends at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity during pregnancy and the first year after birth, and it lists brisk walking as an example [9]. It also says to talk to your doctor about whether or how to adjust your activity during and after pregnancy [9]. Check with your doctor or midwife before starting or changing a routine.

## When should you talk to your doctor?

Brisk walking is low risk for most people. Still, talk to your doctor before ramping up if:

- You have heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, or another long-term condition.
- You take heart or blood pressure medicine. Keep taking it as prescribed.
- You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.
- You're pregnant or recently gave birth.
- You have an injury, joint pain, or a higher risk of falls.

## FAQ

### How fast is a brisk walk?

The CDC defines brisk walking as 2.5 miles per hour or faster [1]. In adults 21 to 40, about 100 steps a minute marks moderate intensity [8].

### Can walking replace running for cardio?

For meeting the weekly guideline, yes. You need about twice as many minutes of moderate activity as vigorous activity [2].

### Do short walks count?

Yes. The CDC says you can break your activity into smaller chunks across the week [2].

### Does slow walking help at all?

Some activity is better than none [2]. But brisk or average-pace walkers had lower heart risk than slow walkers [7].
