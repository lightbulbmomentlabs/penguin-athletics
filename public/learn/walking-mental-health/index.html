Walking and your mind: the complete guide

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 6, 2026 10 min read

The short answer

Walking is good for your mental health. A 2024 review of 75 trials found that walking reduced symptoms of depression and anxiety in adults, and people who move more tend to have a lower risk of depression. Walking works best alongside therapy or medication when you need them, not instead of them.

When your head feels heavy, a walk is one of the easiest things you can try. No gym, no gear, no sign-up. Just you, your shoes, and a bit of fresh air.

The research on walking and mental health has grown a lot in the last few years. It touches depression, anxiety, stress, sleep, focus, and even creative thinking. This guide pulls it together in plain English, with the honest limits included.

One thing up front: walking can help, but it is not a replacement for therapy or medication. If you're getting treatment for a mental health condition, keep going. Think of walking as something you add, not something you swap in.

What does walking do for your mental health?

The U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines list a wide range of brain health benefits from regular moderate-to-vigorous activity like brisk walking. These include a lower risk of depression, less anxiety, better sleep, better quality of life, and improved thinking skills [1].

Some of these benefits show up right after a single session, while others build with regular activity over time [1]. That's good news if you're starting from zero. You don't have to wait months to feel something.

Walking in particular has been studied on its own. A 2024 review pooled 75 randomized trials with 8,636 participants [2]. Compared with people who didn't walk, adults who walked had fewer symptoms of depression and fewer symptoms of anxiety [2].

The benefits held up across many kinds of walking. The review found improvements with different weekly frequencies and walk lengths, indoors and outdoors, and in groups and alone [2]. In other words, there isn't one "right" way to walk for your mind.

Can walking help with depression?

This is where the evidence is strongest. Two kinds of studies point the same way: trials that test walking as a treatment, and large studies that follow people over time.

What the trials show

In the 2024 walking review, walking reduced depressive symptoms in adults whether or not they were depressed at the start [2]. People who were already depressed tended to see bigger improvements than people who weren't [2]. That's an encouraging sign, though only 5 of the trials in that comparison involved people who were depressed, so the estimate is less certain [2].

A large 2024 review in The BMJ looked at exercise as a treatment for major depression. It included 218 trials with 14,170 participants [3]. Walking or jogging led to moderate reductions in depression compared with control conditions such as usual care [3]. The effects were bigger when the exercise was more intense [3].

The authors were careful to note the weak spots. Only one study met the strict criteria for low risk of bias, so confidence in the walking or jogging result was rated low [3]. Still, they concluded that exercise could be considered alongside therapy and antidepressants as a core treatment for depression [3].

What happens over the long run

A 2022 review in JAMA Psychiatry followed more than 191,000 adults in 15 studies [4]. People who got activity equal to about 2.5 hours of brisk walking a week had a 25% lower risk of depression than people who reported no activity [4].

Even half that amount, about 1.25 hours of brisk walking a week, was linked to an 18% lower risk [4]. The biggest differences showed up when people went from no activity to some activity [4]. Beyond the recommended amount, the extra benefits got smaller and less certain [4].

These were observational studies. They show a link, not proof that walking prevents depression. People who feel well may also find it easier to be active.

What about steps?

If you track steps, this one's for you. A 2024 review of 33 studies with 96,173 adults looked at daily step counts and depression [5].

In the long-term studies, people who took 7,000 or more steps a day had a 31% lower risk of depression than people who took fewer than 7,000 [5]. Each extra 1,000 steps a day was linked to a 9% lower risk [5]. Most of the included studies took a snapshot at a single point in time, and the authors said more long-term research is needed [5].

Can walking replace therapy or medication?

No. The research supports walking as a helpful addition, not a substitute. The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) says depression treatment typically involves psychotherapy, medication, or both [6].

NIMH also lists physical activity as something you can try to help yourself, noting that just 30 minutes a day of walking can boost your mood [6]. If you take medication, don't start or stop it on your own. Talk to a health care provider before making any changes [6].

Does walking help with anxiety and stress?

Yes, the evidence suggests it does. In the 2024 walking review, walking reduced anxiety symptoms in adults compared with not walking [2]. The effect was a bit smaller than for depression, but it was still clear [2].

A large overview of 97 reviews, covering 1,039 trials and 128,119 people, looked at physical activity in general [7]. Activity had medium-sized benefits for depression, anxiety, and psychological distress compared with usual care [7]. Higher-intensity activity tended to bring bigger improvements [7].

The Physical Activity Guidelines describe two kinds of anxiety relief. Right after a session of moderate-to-vigorous activity, people tend to feel less short-term anxiety [1]. With regular activity, longer-term anxiety also improves, both in people with and without anxiety disorders [1].

There is less research on stress as its own outcome. The closest measure, psychological distress, improved with physical activity in the large overview [7].

Can walking help you sleep better?

Probably, yes. Sleep and mood are tightly linked, so this matters for your mind too.

According to the Physical Activity Guidelines, adults who are more active sleep better [1]. More moderate-to-vigorous activity is linked to falling asleep faster, more time in bed actually asleep, better sleep quality, and more deep sleep [1]. It's also linked to less daytime sleepiness and less use of sleep medicine [1].

A meta-analysis of 66 studies on exercise and sleep found that regular exercise had small benefits for total sleep time and sleep efficiency [8]. It had moderate benefits for overall sleep quality [8]. This review looked at exercise in general, not walking alone, but walking is a common way people get that activity.

If you live with insomnia or another sleep problem, talk to your doctor. Walking can be part of the plan, but it may not be the whole plan.

Does walking help focus, memory, and creativity?

Creativity

One of the most fun findings comes from a set of four experiments. Walking boosted creative thinking both during the walk and shortly after [9]. In the first experiment, 81% of people scored higher on a creative brainstorming test while walking than while sitting [9].

The boost carried over when people sat down again after walking [9]. Walking outside produced the most original ideas [9]. So if you're stuck on a problem, a short walk might help you get unstuck.

Focus and memory

The Physical Activity Guidelines report that a single session of moderate-to-vigorous activity can improve some thinking skills right away [1]. Regular activity is linked to better executive function, the set of skills you use to plan, organize, and stay on task [1].

For memory, one well-known trial followed 120 older adults [10]. Aerobic exercise training increased the size of the hippocampus, a brain area important for memory, by 2% [10]. That growth came with better spatial memory [10]. The study tested aerobic exercise for older adults, so the results may not apply to everyone.

Does it matter where and with whom you walk?

Nature or city?

Both seem to help. In the 2024 walking review, walking reduced symptoms of depression or anxiety both indoors and outdoors [2]. A treadmill walk on a rainy day still counts.

That said, green space may add something. In a Stanford study of healthy adults, a 90-minute walk in a natural setting reduced rumination, the habit of replaying negative thoughts over and over [11]. A 90-minute walk in an urban setting did not [11]. The nature walk also lowered activity in a brain area linked to rumination [11].

This was one small study, so don't treat it as the final word. But if you have a park, a trail, or even a tree-lined street nearby, it may be worth the detour.

Alone or with others?

Again, both work. The walking review found benefits for group walking and for walking alone [2].

A review of outdoor walking groups included 42 studies with 1,843 people [12]. Walking groups reduced depression scores, and the studies reported no notable harmful side effects [12]. Walking with others also gives you something many people need when they're struggling: a bit of connection, and someone expecting you to show up.

How quickly do the benefits show up?

Some benefits can start the same day. Others take weeks.

Right away. After a single session of moderate-to-vigorous activity, people tend to feel less short-term anxiety and see improvements in some thinking skills and in sleep [1]. Creative thinking gets a boost during a walk and shortly after [9]. In the nature study, one 90-minute walk was enough to reduce rumination [11].

Over weeks to months. The trials in the 2024 walking review lasted anywhere from 10 days to 18 months, and most ran 3 to 6 months [2]. Interestingly, programs shorter than 3 months showed stronger effects than programs lasting 3 to 6 months [2].

The large overview found something similar. Programs of 12 weeks or less had bigger effects on depression than longer programs [7]. The authors suggested that people may stick with shorter programs more easily [7]. Either way, you don't need to commit to a year to notice a difference.

Mental health doesn't improve in a straight line. Some days a walk will help a lot, and some days it won't seem to do anything. That's normal. Keep going, and pay attention to the trend over a few weeks.

How much walking do you need?

Less than you might think. The U.S. guidelines recommend 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate activity, like brisk walking, for substantial health benefits [1]. That's about 30 minutes a day, five days a week.

But the same guidelines say some activity is better than none [1]. For depression, the biggest drop in risk came from going from nothing to something, and half the recommended amount was still linked to an 18% lower risk [4].

Pace matters a little too. In both large reviews, more intense activity was linked to bigger mental health benefits [3] [7]. Brisk walking, where you can talk but not sing, is a good target. If that feels like too much right now, any walking is a fine place to start.

How to start walking for your mental health

Starting is often the hardest part, especially when your mood is low. Keep it small and kind. Try this:

  1. Start with 10 minutes. A short walk around the block counts. Some activity is better than none [1].
  2. Pick a time and attach it to a habit. Walk after your morning coffee or after lunch. Routine does the deciding for you on low-energy days.
  3. Build toward 30 minutes a day. Add a few minutes each week until you reach about 150 minutes a week [1].
  4. Speed up when you're ready. A brisk pace tends to bring bigger benefits [3].
  5. Find some green. A park or tree-lined route may help quiet looping thoughts [11].
  6. Bring someone. A friend, a family member, or a local walking group can make it easier to show up [12].
  7. Notice how you feel. Rate your mood before and after a few walks. Seeing a small lift can be the reason you go again tomorrow.
  8. Count it. A step counter or walking app lets you see your days add up, which can help on days you don't feel like going.

When should you get help?

Walking is a helpful tool, but it isn't enough on its own for everyone. Talk to a doctor or mental health professional if:

  • Low mood, hopelessness, or numbness sticks around or keeps coming back.
  • Worry or panic gets in the way of work, school, sleep, or relationships.
  • You've lost interest in things you used to enjoy.
  • You're using alcohol or other substances to cope.
  • You're already in treatment and things aren't getting better.

Depression is treatable, and treatment usually involves therapy, medication, or both [6]. If you take medication for your mental health, keep taking it as prescribed. Don't stop or change it without talking to your provider first [6]. Walking can sit alongside your treatment, and your care team can help you fit it in.

If you're in crisis or thinking about suicide, please reach out now. In the U.S., you can call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline [6]. You can also chat online through 988lifeline.org [13]. It's free, confidential, and available 24/7 [13]. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911 [6].

FAQ

Is walking good for depression?

Yes, as part of a wider plan. Trials show walking reduces depressive symptoms in adults [2]. It works best alongside therapy or medication when those are needed, not instead of them [6].

How long should I walk to feel better?

Even one brisk walk can ease short-term anxiety [1]. For lasting benefits, build toward about 30 minutes a day, most days of the week [1].

Is walking outside better than a treadmill?

Both help with depression and anxiety symptoms [2]. Walking in nature may give an extra lift, especially for repetitive negative thoughts [11].

How many steps a day help your mood?

In long-term studies, 7,000 or more steps a day was linked to a lower risk of depression [5]. Every extra 1,000 steps a day was linked to a slightly lower risk [5].
