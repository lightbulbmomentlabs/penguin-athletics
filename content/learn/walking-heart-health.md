Your heart beats around the clock, and it does better when the rest of you moves. Walking is the easiest way to give it that movement. No gym, no gear, no learning curve.

It's also one of the most studied habits in all of heart health. Researchers have tested walking in dozens of controlled trials [2] and followed hundreds of thousands of people for years to see who develops heart disease [5].

This guide pulls that research together. You'll learn what walking does for your blood pressure, cholesterol, heart rate, and blood vessels, how much and how fast to walk, who benefits, and how to start safely, even if you already have a heart condition.

## What does walking do for your heart?

When you walk regularly, your heart muscle gets stronger. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) explains that regular moderate activity helps your heart pump blood to your lungs and the rest of your body [1]. More blood reaches your muscles, and the oxygen in your blood rises [1].

Regular activity also works on many of the things that raise your risk of heart disease. According to the NHLBI, physical activity can help by [1]:

- Lowering blood pressure and triglycerides (a type of fat in the blood)
- Raising HDL ("good") cholesterol
- Helping your body manage blood sugar and insulin
- Lowering C-reactive protein, a sign of inflammation linked to heart disease

The NHLBI also notes that inactive people are more likely to develop heart disease than active people [1]. Studies suggest inactivity is a major risk factor, just like high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and smoking [1].

Those points cover physical activity in general. The sections below look at what studies of *walking* specifically have found, because that's the question you care about.

## Does walking lower blood pressure?

Yes, and this is where the evidence for walking is strongest.

A 2021 Cochrane review pooled 73 randomized trials with 5,763 people in 22 countries [2]. Walking lowered systolic blood pressure (the top number) by about **4.1 mmHg** compared with not walking [2]. The researchers rated this as moderate-certainty evidence, meaning walking probably lowers it [2].

Diastolic pressure (the bottom number) fell by about **1.8 mmHg**, though that evidence was lower certainty [2]. The benefit showed up across age groups, from people 40 and under to people over 60, and in both women and men [2].

Another review looked only at walking trials in healthy but inactive adults. It included 37 trials and 2,001 people [3]. Walking improved both systolic and diastolic blood pressure, along with fitness and fasting blood sugar [3].

A few points may not sound like much. But high blood pressure is a risk factor for coronary heart disease, and lowering it is one way activity helps your heart [1]. We cover the details, including how fast it works, in [Does walking lower blood pressure?](/learn/does-walking-lower-blood-pressure/)

## Does walking lower cholesterol?

This is where the evidence gets mixed, and it's worth being honest about.

Physical activity in general can lower triglycerides and raise HDL ("good") cholesterol, according to the NHLBI [1]. But trials of walking alone show smaller and less consistent effects on blood fats.

An older review of 25 walking trials found that walking lowered LDL ("bad") cholesterol by about **5%** [4]. It also improved the ratio of total cholesterol to HDL by about 6% [4]. It didn't find clear changes in total cholesterol, HDL, or triglycerides, although those moved in a helpful direction [4].

The more recent review of 37 walking trials found no significant effect on the four blood fat measures it studied [3].

So what does that mean for you? Walking is good for your heart in many ways, but don't count on it alone to fix high cholesterol. If your cholesterol is high, your doctor may suggest other steps, like changes to what you eat or medicine. Walking works well alongside those, not instead of them.

## Does walking lower your risk of heart disease and stroke?

Trials can measure blood pressure over a few months. But heart attacks and strokes build up over years, so for those, researchers follow large groups of people over time. These studies show strong links, though they can't prove walking is the only cause.

### People who walk more tend to have fewer heart problems

A well-known review pooled 18 long-term studies with more than 459,000 people who were free of heart disease at the start [5]. People in the highest walking group had about a **31% lower risk** of cardiovascular disease than people in the lowest group [5].

The researchers saw a dose-response pattern: more walking went with lower risk [5]. They also found signs of publication bias for the heart disease results, which means the true effect may be somewhat smaller [5].

### Steps tell a similar story

Newer studies measure steps with devices instead of asking people how much they walk [6].

A 2023 analysis of 8 studies tracked more than 20,000 adults for about 6 years [6]. Heart disease, stroke, and heart failure were all counted as cardiovascular events [6]. In adults 60 and older, those who took the most steps had about half the risk of a cardiovascular event compared with those who took the fewest [6]. The study didn't find a clear link in adults under 60 [6].

Another 2023 review of 17 studies and nearly 227,000 people found that every extra 500 steps a day was linked to a **7% lower risk** of dying from cardiovascular disease [7].

## Does walking lower your resting heart rate?

Probably a little. In the Cochrane review, regular walking lowered resting heart rate by about **2.8 beats per minute** compared with not walking [2]. That evidence was rated low certainty, so treat it as likely, not guaranteed [2].

A lower resting heart rate fits with what the NHLBI describes: regular moderate activity strengthens your heart muscle and helps it pump blood more effectively [1].

Don't worry if your own number barely moves. It's one sign among many, not a scorecard.

## How does walking help your blood vessels and circulation?

Your heart is only half the system. Your blood vessels matter just as much.

When you exercise, including walking, more blood flows through your arteries. Researchers have found that exercise changes blood flow and the pressure on artery walls in ways that can change how arteries work, and even their size and wall thickness [8]. These changes are thought to help explain why active people have lower heart risk [8]. They may affect the build-up of plaque in large arteries and help control blood pressure in smaller ones [8].

Activity also affects the tiniest vessels. The NHLBI notes that capillaries widen with regular activity, so they can deliver more oxygen and carry away waste [1].

### Walking and leg pain from narrowed arteries

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) narrows the arteries in the legs. It can cause leg pain when walking, called intermittent claudication [9].

Exercise is a low-cost, low-risk option for this kind of leg pain [9]. A Cochrane review of 32 trials with 1,835 people found that exercise programs, which included walking, helped people walk farther [9]. On average, they could walk about **82 meters farther** before pain started and about **120 meters farther** in total [9]. The evidence was rated high quality, and the benefits lasted up to two years [9].

If you have PAD, ask your doctor about a supervised exercise program. Don't try to push through severe pain on your own.

## How much walking is good for your heart?

The CDC recommends that adults get **150 minutes a week** of moderate activity, such as brisk walking [10]. That's about 30 minutes a day, five days a week. The CDC also recommends muscle-strengthening activity on at least 2 days a week [10].

Here's the good news. You don't have to do it all at once. The CDC says you can spread your activity across the week and break it into smaller chunks [10]. Two 15-minute walks count. So do three 10-minute walks.

The CDC also says some physical activity is better than none [10]. The step studies back this up: benefits started well below the famous 10,000-step mark [7].

The walking programs in the Cochrane review averaged about **153 minutes a week**, mostly at moderate intensity, for about 15 weeks [2]. So the CDC target matches what worked in the trials.

One honest caveat: researchers still can't say exactly how often, how long, or how hard you need to walk to improve each heart risk factor [3]. The 150-minute target is a solid, well-tested goal, not a magic number.

## Does walking speed matter for your heart?

It seems to. Faster walking is linked to lower heart risk, even when the total amount of walking is similar.

In the review of 18 long-term studies, walking pace was a stronger predictor of lower risk than the amount of walking [5]. Pace was tied to a 48% lower overall risk, compared with 26% for walking volume [5].

A pooled study of more than 50,000 walkers in Britain found something similar [11]. Compared with people who said they walked slowly, those who walked at an average pace had a 24% lower risk of dying from cardiovascular disease [11]. Those who walked at a brisk or fast pace had a 21% lower risk [11]. These are links, not proof, and people judged their own pace.

### How fast is "brisk"?

The CDC counts walking at **2.5 miles per hour or faster** as moderate-intensity activity [12]. You don't need a speedometer, though. Try the talk test: at moderate intensity, you can talk but not sing [12].

If you can only say a few words before you need a breath, you've reached vigorous intensity [12]. That's fine for some people, but you don't need it to help your heart.

## Who benefits from walking for heart health?

Almost everyone. In the Cochrane review, people from 16 to 84 years old took part, and walking lowered blood pressure in every age group [2].

A few groups may have extra reasons to start:

- **People who are mostly inactive.** The walking trials focused on inactive adults, and they still saw benefits in blood pressure, fitness, and blood sugar [3].
- **Older adults.** In the step-count study, the link between more steps and lower heart risk was clearest in people 60 and older [6].
- **People with rising blood pressure.** Walking lowered blood pressure across the trials, so it's a useful habit to build early [2].
- **People with heart disease.** Exercise is a core part of cardiac rehab, as you'll see below [13].

## Is walking safe if you have a heart condition?

For most people, yes, and it's often encouraged. The NHLBI says the benefits of regular physical activity far outweigh the risks to the heart and lungs [14]. Heart problems caused by activity are rare [14].

That said, the risk isn't zero. According to the NHLBI, the risk is related to how fit you are and how hard you're exercising [14]. Middle-aged and older adults with coronary heart disease are more likely to have a heart attack when exercising vigorously [14]. The NHLBI notes that vigorous activity may not be safe for people with heart disease, so ask your doctor what's safe for you [1].

### Walking after a heart attack or heart procedure

Cardiac rehabilitation is a program built around exercise training, sometimes combined with education and emotional support [13]. A 2026 Cochrane review of 107 trials with 26,886 adults with coronary heart disease looked at exercise-based cardiac rehab [13].

Within 6 to 12 months, exercise-based rehab led to a large reduction in heart attacks (about 28% fewer) [13]. It likely reduced hospital stays for any reason and likely led to a small drop in deaths [13]. It also improved quality of life [13].

Those results are for structured exercise programs as a whole, not walking by itself. If you've had a heart attack, heart surgery, or a stent, ask your doctor whether cardiac rehab is right for you.

### Talk to your doctor first if any of these apply

- You have heart disease, heart failure, or an irregular heartbeat.
- You've had a heart attack, stroke, heart surgery, or a stent.
- You have high blood pressure, diabetes, or another long-term condition.
- You take medicine for your heart or blood pressure.
- You've had chest pain, dizziness, or fainting during activity.

The NHLBI advises anyone with a heart problem or long-term disease to ask their doctor what types of activity are safe for them [14]. You can also ask how to build up slowly and safely [14]. If you take medicine, keep taking it as prescribed. Walking works alongside treatment, not instead of it.

### Know the warning signs

Talk with your doctor if you get chest pain or dizziness during activity [14]. Stop walking and call 911 right away if you notice signs of a heart attack [15], such as:

- Chest pain or discomfort
- Feeling weak, light-headed, or faint
- Pain or discomfort in the jaw, neck, back, arms, or shoulders
- Shortness of breath

## How to start walking for your heart

You don't need a perfect plan. You need a plan you'll repeat. Try this:

1. **Check in first if you need to.** If anything in the doctor list above applies to you, start there [14].
2. **Start small.** If you're not active now, begin with 10 minutes a day. Some activity is better than none [10].
3. **Add a few minutes each week.** Build toward 150 minutes a week, about 30 minutes on most days [10].
4. **Break it up.** Short walks count [10]. Try 10 minutes after meals.
5. **Pick up the pace over time.** Aim for a speed where you can talk but not sing [12].
6. **Give it a few months.** The walking trials lasted about 15 weeks on average [2]. Your blood pressure may change before you notice anything else.
7. **Track your walks.** A step counter or walking app makes progress easy to see, and seeing it helps you keep going.
8. **Add strength twice a week.** Simple moves like squats or wall push-ups round out the CDC's advice [10].

## FAQ

### Is walking as good as running for your heart?

Brisk walking counts as real exercise. The CDC lists brisk walking as moderate activity toward the 150-minute weekly goal [10], while running counts as vigorous [12]. Walking is a great choice if running isn't for you. For the head-to-head research, see [walking vs running: which is better for your heart?](/learn/walking-vs-running-which-is-better-for-your-heart/)

### Can walking replace heart medicine?

No. Walking works alongside treatment, not instead of it. Keep taking your medicine as prescribed, and ask your doctor what activity is safe for you [14].

### Is it okay to walk every day?

Yes. Daily walking is a great way to reach 150 minutes a week [10]. If you have a heart condition, ask your doctor how much is right for you [14].

### What's a good walking pace for heart health?

Brisk: about 2.5 miles per hour or faster, where you can talk but not sing [12]. Faster walkers tend to have lower heart risk [5].
