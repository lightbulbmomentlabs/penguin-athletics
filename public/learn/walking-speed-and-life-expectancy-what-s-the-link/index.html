Walking speed and life expectancy: what's the link?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 17, 2026 6 min read

The short answer

People who naturally walk faster tend to live longer. In a pooled study of 34,485 adults 65 and older, each 0.1 meter per second of extra walking speed was linked to a 12% lower risk of death, and speeds of about 1.0 meter per second (around 2.2 mph) or faster went with longer-than-expected survival. Speed is partly a sign of overall health, so it shows a strong link rather than proof that walking faster adds years.

How fast you walk to the mailbox says more about you than you'd think. Researchers who time people's normal walking speed, then follow them for years, keep finding the same pattern: faster walkers tend to live longer.

But there's a twist. Walking speed is partly a signal of how healthy you already are, not just a habit you can change. So the real questions are what your speed can tell you, and whether speeding up on purpose helps. Here's what the research shows. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to walking and longevity.)

What does the research show?

The landmark study pooled data from 9 groups of 34,485 adults aged 65 and older, who were followed for 6 to 21 years. Everyone was timed walking at their usual pace from a standing start.

Faster walking speed was linked to longer survival in every one of the 9 studies. Each 0.1 meter per second faster (a small difference) was linked to a 12% lower risk of death. Survival kept rising across the whole range of speeds, with no single cutoff.

The differences were large. At age 75, predicted 10-year survival ranged from 19% to 87% in men and 35% to 91% in women, depending on walking speed. For 70-year-old men, predicted life expectancy ranged from 7 to 23 more years, and for women from 10 to 30 years.

Newer reviews point the same way:

  • A 2024 meta-analysis of 13 studies and 530,841 people found the fastest walkers had a 43% lower risk of death than the slowest. Every 0.1 meter per second faster was linked to a 6% lower risk.
  • A 2025 review of 48 studies found the slowest walkers had a 49% higher risk of death than the fastest. Slow walking was also linked to higher risks of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and cancer.

How fast is "fast enough"?

In the big 2011 study, a speed of about 0.8 meters per second (roughly 1.8 mph) lined up with average life expectancy for a person's age and sex. Speeds of 1.0 meter per second or faster (about 2.2 mph) consistently went with longer survival than expected.

The authors suggested that faster than 1.0 meter per second may signal better-than-average life expectancy, and above 1.2 meters per second (about 2.7 mph) may signal exceptional life expectancy. They noted that more research is needed to confirm those cutoffs.

For comparison, the CDC lists walking briskly at 2.5 mph or faster as moderate-intensity activity. That's a bit over 1.1 meters per second, so a normal brisk walk lands above the 1.0 mark.

What about how fast you think you walk?

Many big studies simply ask people to rate their usual pace as slow, average, or brisk. Those answers turn out to matter.

In about 475,000 UK adults, people who said they walked briskly had estimated life expectancies of 86.7 to 87.8 years for women and 85.2 to 86.8 years for men, across all body sizes. People who said they walked slowly had shorter life expectancies.

In another UK study of about 420,000 adults without heart disease or cancer, slow walkers had roughly twice the risk of death of brisk walkers in some groups. Self-rated slow pace was also linked to higher risk of dying from heart disease.

Does speed cause a longer life, or just reflect it?

Probably both, to some degree. But the "reflects it" part is big.

Walking puts demands on your heart, lungs, blood vessels, nerves, muscles, and joints all at once. When any of those systems is struggling, walking tends to slow down. That's why researchers have called walking speed "a simple and accessible summary indicator of vitality".

So a slow pace can be an early sign that something else is going on. One UK study noted that this kind of reverse effect (illness causing slow walking, not the other way around) remained a possible explanation for its findings.

Is there evidence that speeding up helps?

There are hints, though no trial has proven it yet.

  • In 439 adults aged 65 and older, those whose usual walking speed improved by 0.1 meter per second over a year had lower death rates over the next 8 years. Death rates were 31.6% for people who improved, compared with 49.3% for those who never improved. The authors said more research is needed to see whether raising someone's speed changes survival.
  • A pooled study of 50,225 British walkers found that an average or brisk pace was linked to a 20% and 24% lower risk of death, compared with a slow pace. The authors wrote that, if the link is causal, walking faster could lower risk.
  • A genetic study in about 406,000 UK adults found that average and brisk walkers had longer telomeres, the protective caps on chromosomes that tend to shorten with age. Its genetic analysis suggested that a faster pace may lead to longer telomeres, and not the other way around. Telomere length has been proposed as a marker of biological age. It isn't a direct measure of how long you'll live.

Put together: your natural speed is a useful health signal, and adding brisk walking is a reasonable bet. But it's not proof that speed alone adds years.

Does speed matter if you already get your steps?

It may add a little. A large study of British adults found that brisk walking was linked to lower risk among people who didn't meet activity recommendations or didn't do vigorous exercise. The authors suggested that walking pace could be a useful focus when walking more often or for longer isn't realistic.

Studies that track steps with a device show more mixed results on pace once total steps are counted. We cover that in how many steps a day to live longer. The short version: get your steps first, then make some of them brisk.

How to check and build your walking speed

You don't need a lab. Researchers often time a short walk of about 4 meters with a stopwatch. Here's a simple version to try at home:

  1. Measure a straight path. Mark about 4 meters (13 feet) in a hallway or on a sidewalk.
  2. Walk it at your normal pace. Start from standing, don't rush, and time yourself. Do it twice and use the faster time.
  3. Do the math. Divide 4 by your time in seconds. 4 seconds equals 1.0 meter per second.
  4. Add brisk minutes. Aim for a pace where you can talk but not sing. Start with a few faster minutes in the middle of a regular walk.
  5. Build toward the weekly goal. Adults 65 and older are advised to get 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, like brisk walking, plus muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days and balance activities.
  6. Don't forget strength and balance. Strong legs and good balance help you walk faster and more safely.
  7. Recheck every few months. Look for a trend, not a perfect number.

When should you talk to your doctor?

A slow or slowing walking speed is worth mentioning to your doctor, especially if it changed without a clear reason. It can reflect issues that are worth checking.

Also talk to your doctor before walking faster if:

  • You have heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, or lung disease.
  • You've fallen recently or feel unsteady on your feet.
  • You have joint pain or an injury that gets worse when you walk.
  • You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath during activity.

If you can't meet the full recommendations, the CDC advises being as active as your abilities and conditions allow. Some activity is better than none. (For heart-specific advice, see how many steps a day you need for heart health.)

FAQ

What is a normal walking speed?

In the big 2011 study, adults 65 and older averaged about 0.92 meters per second. About 0.8 lined up with average life expectancy, and 1.0 or faster with longer survival.

Is walking speed a vital sign?

Some researchers call it one. Authors of the 2007 study said it "may be a useful 'vital sign'" for older adults.

Can a slow walker still live long?

Yes. These are averages across large groups, not predictions for one person. Many things affect lifespan, and the CDC notes that some activity is better than none.

Does a treadmill speed count?

The studies timed people's usual pace over ground. A treadmill can help you practice brisk walking, but its numbers may not match a hallway test.
