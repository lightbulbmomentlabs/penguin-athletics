Most walking research is about people in their 60s and 70s. So it's fair to wonder if a lunch walk at 27 really matters, or if you can just start caring at 50.

A few long-running studies have followed people from their 20s into middle age. They point the same way: the fitness and habits you build now are linked to how your heart, weight, and brain are doing decades later.

Here's what that research found, what it can't prove, and how to make walking stick through a busy decade. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to [walking and longevity](/learn/walking-longevity/).)

## What happens decades later if you're fit in your 20s?

The best evidence comes from the CARDIA study, which began in 1985 with thousands of U.S. adults aged 18 to 30. Researchers measured their fitness with a treadmill test, then kept checking in for decades.

In one analysis of 4,872 of those young adults, followed for a median of about 27 years, each extra minute on the baseline treadmill test was linked to a **15% lower risk of death** and a **12% lower risk of cardiovascular disease**, such as heart attack and stroke [1].

A newer CARDIA analysis looked at 4,808 people, with an average starting age of about 25, and more than half of them women [2]. Each extra minute on the treadmill at the start was linked to a **27% lower risk of dying for women** and a 13% lower risk for men, counting deaths from their mid-40s onward [2].

### Does it matter if your fitness slips?

Yes. In the first analysis, losing one minute of treadmill time over the first seven years was linked to a 21% higher risk of death and a 20% higher risk of cardiovascular disease [1]. In the newer one, every 5% more of your early fitness that you kept into midlife was linked to an 11% lower risk of death [2].

In other words, being fit at 25 helps, but keeping it through your 30s and 40s seems to matter too.

## Does walking actually build that fitness?

Treadmill time in these studies is a measure of cardiorespiratory fitness, which is how well your heart and lungs supply your muscles. Walking is one of the easiest ways to raise it.

A review of 32 randomized trials in inactive adults found that walking programs **increased aerobic fitness**, lowered blood pressure, and trimmed waist size and body fat [3]. The changes were modest, but they came from walking alone, with no other exercise added [3].

One honest caveat: CARDIA measured fitness, not walking specifically. No study has followed people's step counts from age 25 to 55. So the chain here is that walking builds fitness, and fitness in young adulthood is linked to better long-term health. ## Does staying active now affect your weight later?

Weight tends to creep up from your 20s into middle age, and staying active seems to slow that down, especially for women.

Over 20 years in the CARDIA study, women who kept up high activity levels from young adulthood gained **6.1 fewer kilograms** (about 13 pounds) than women with low activity [4]. Their waistlines grew 3.8 centimeters less [4]. For men, the difference was smaller, at 2.6 kilograms [4]. The researchers concluded that staying highly active through young adulthood may lessen weight gain, "particularly in women" [4].

This isn't about chasing a number on the scale. Steady habits in your 20s and 30s are simply linked to an easier path through midlife.

## Can it help your brain in your 40s and 50s?

There's a link here too. In 2,747 CARDIA participants, people who were fitter at ages 18 to 30 did better on memory and thinking-speed tests 25 years later, at ages 43 to 55 [5]. The fittest quarter scored modestly higher than the least fit quarter on every test [5].

People whose fitness dropped less over 20 years also did better on a test of processing speed [5]. Like the other CARDIA findings, this is observational, so it shows a link rather than proof.

## What about your bones?

This matters especially for women. According to the National Institutes of Health, the amount of bone in most people's skeleton peaks by their **mid- to late 20s** [6]. Women have lower peak bone mass and smaller bones than men [7], so the bone you build and keep now matters.

The NIH lists brisk walking at 3 to 4 miles per hour as a weight-bearing exercise, the kind that makes bones work harder [8]. It says exercise prevents bone loss in adults, and that mixing weight-bearing activity with strength training is best for building and keeping healthy bones [8].

So walking in your 20s and 30s counts toward your bones, but it works best alongside some strength training.

## Is it too late if you weren't active in your 20s?

No. A U.S. study of more than 315,000 adults asked people how active they had been at different ages, starting in their teens [9]. People who stayed active from their teens through midlife had a **36% lower risk of dying** during the study than people who were inactive throughout [9].

But people who had been inactive for most of adulthood and picked up activity between 40 and 61 had a similar 35% lower risk [9]. The researchers concluded that midlife is not too late to start, and that young adults who are already active should try to keep it up [9]. (More in [is it too late to start walking at 60?](/learn/is-it-too-late-to-start-walking-at-60/))

These results rely on people remembering how active they were decades ago, so they're not exact. But the message is encouraging either way: starting now helps, and so does not stopping.

## How to keep walking through a busy decade

Your 20s and 30s bring job changes, moves, late nights, and sometimes a baby. The goal isn't a perfect streak. It's not letting your fitness quietly slide. Try this:

1. **Know the target.** Adults need at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, like brisk walking, such as 30 minutes on 5 days [10].
2. **Attach it to your commute or lunch.** Get off a stop early, walk to grab lunch, or take calls on foot. It's easier to keep a walk you don't have to schedule.
3. **Make some of it brisk.** Brisk walking is the CDC's example of moderate activity, the level that counts toward the guidelines [10].
4. **Add strength twice a week.** Adults also need muscle-strengthening activity 2 days a week [10], which helps your bones too [8].
5. **Have a "minimum" walk for chaotic weeks.** A short walk still adds to your weekly minutes, and keeping some of your fitness beats losing all of it [2].
6. **Track your average, not your best day.** Watching your weekly trend makes slow slips easier to catch. Our guide to [a good daily step goal](/learn/what-is-a-good-daily-step-goal/) can help you pick a number.

If exercise has never been your thing, start with [how to start walking when you hate exercise](/learn/how-to-start-walking-when-you-hate-exercise/).

## When should you talk to your doctor first?

Walking is safe for most people, but check with your doctor first if:

- You're pregnant or recently gave birth. The CDC recommends at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, like brisk walking, during pregnancy and postpartum, and says to talk to your doctor about whether or how to adjust [11].
- You have heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, or another long-term condition.
- You have low bone density, osteoporosis, or a physical limitation. The NIH advises talking to a health care provider before starting an exercise program [8].
- You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.

## FAQ

### Is walking enough exercise in your 20s?

Brisk walking counts toward the 150 weekly minutes adults need [10]. Add strength training 2 days a week to round it out [10].

### Do I need to walk fast for it to count?

Some of it should be. Brisk walking is what counts as moderate activity in the guidelines [10].

### Does fitness from my 20s last forever?

No. In CARDIA, people whose fitness dropped over time had higher risk, and those who kept more of it did better [1][2].

### Does walking build bone in your 20s?

Brisk walking is weight-bearing, and the NIH says exercise helps prevent bone loss in adults [8]. Pairing it with strength training is best for bones [8].
