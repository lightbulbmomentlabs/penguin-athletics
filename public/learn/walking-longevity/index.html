Walking and longevity: the complete guide

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 6, 2026 11 min read

The short answer

People who walk more tend to live longer. In a 2025 review of 57 studies, 7,000 steps a day was linked to a 47% lower risk of early death compared with 2,000 steps, and the benefits started to level off around 5,000 to 7,000 steps. Most of this evidence is observational, so it shows a strong link rather than proof, but walking is also linked to lower dementia risk and better function as you age.

Walking may be one of the simplest ways to add healthy years to your life. Over the last decade, step-counting devices have let researchers track how much people really move, then follow them for years.

The results are surprisingly consistent. People who take more steps each day tend to live longer, keep their thinking sharper, and stay independent for longer. You also need fewer steps than the famous 10,000 to see much of that benefit.

This guide pulls the best research together in one place: how many steps matter, how fast to walk, what walking does for your brain and your balance, and how to make it stick at any age.

Does walking really help you live longer?

The short answer is yes, as far as the research can tell. But it helps to know what kind of research we're talking about.

Most studies on walking and lifespan are observational. Researchers measure how much people walk, then track who dies over the following years. These studies can show that walking more is linked to living longer. They can't fully prove walking is the cause, because people who walk more may also differ in other ways, like being healthier to begin with. Good studies adjust for those differences, but they can't remove them all.

With that caveat, the evidence is large and points in one direction.

A 2025 review in The Lancet Public Health pooled 57 studies from 35 groups of people around the world [1]. Compared with 2,000 steps a day, walking 7,000 steps a day was linked to a 47% lower risk of death from any cause during the studies [1]. The same step count was also linked to a 25% lower risk of developing heart disease and a 37% lower risk of dying from cancer [1]. The researchers rated the certainty of the evidence as moderate for most outcomes [1].

An earlier analysis pooled raw data from 15 studies and more than 47,000 adults [2]. The people in the most active quarter, with a median of about 10,900 steps a day, had a 53% lower risk of dying during follow-up than people in the least active quarter, who took about 3,550 steps [2].

How many steps a day do you need?

Here's the encouraging part. The biggest gains seem to come from going from very few steps to a moderate number. After that, the benefit keeps growing for a while, then flattens out.

Small increases count

A 2023 review of 12 studies and more than 111,000 people found that the risk of death was already measurably lower at around 2,500 steps a day, compared with 2,000 [3]. The researchers concluded that as few as about 2,600 steps a day was linked to a real benefit [3].

In the large pooled analysis, simply moving from the lowest quarter (about 3,550 steps) to the second quarter (about 5,800 steps) was linked to a 40% lower risk of death [2].

That fits the U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines, which say that any amount of physical activity has some health benefits [4]. If you're barely moving now, your first extra 1,000 or 2,000 steps may matter more than anything that comes later.

Where the benefits level off

Most studies find a curve, not a straight line. The risk keeps dropping as steps go up, then levels off:

  • In the 2025 review, the curve for early death bent at around 5,000 to 7,000 steps a day [1].
  • In the 2023 review, the biggest reduction in risk was at about 8,800 steps a day [3].
  • In a study of nearly 17,000 older women with an average age of 72, the risk of death dropped with more steps until about 7,500 steps a day, then leveled off [5].

Leveling off doesn't mean extra steps are useless. It means each additional step seems to buy a smaller gain once you're already fairly active.

How it differs by age

Age seems to change where the curve flattens. In the 15-study analysis, adults 60 and older saw their risk keep falling up to about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day [2]. Adults younger than 60 kept seeing lower risk up to about 8,000 to 10,000 steps a day [2].

So a realistic goal for many older adults may be lower than the one a 30-year-old would aim for. The 2025 review noted that it couldn't look at age groups separately, so this question still needs more research [1].

Is 10,000 steps a day a myth?

Not a myth, exactly. Just not a magic number.

Researchers have pointed out that the 10,000-step goal is widely believed but has a limited scientific basis [5]. The newer studies suggest the benefits for lifespan mostly level off below 10,000 steps for many people, especially older adults [1][2][5].

The 2025 review put it this way: 10,000 steps can still be a fine target for people who are already active, but 7,000 steps a day is linked to meaningful health benefits and may be a more realistic goal for some [1].

If you already hit 10,000, keep going. Walking more is still linked to good things, like a lower risk of type 2 diabetes and depression symptoms, which kept improving in a straight line with more steps in that review [1]. If 10,000 feels out of reach, don't let it stop you. A goal you can actually meet beats one you abandon.

Does walking speed matter?

There are two different questions here. Does your natural walking speed say something about your health? And does walking faster on purpose help you live longer?

Walking speed as a health signal

How fast you naturally walk turns out to be a strong signal of overall health as you age. A landmark analysis pooled data from 9 studies and 34,485 adults aged 65 and older [6]. Faster walking speed was linked to longer survival in every study [6].

Each 0.1 meter-per-second increase in walking speed (a small difference) was linked to a 12% lower risk of death [6]. At age 75, predicted 10-year survival ranged from 19% to 87% in men and from 35% to 91% in women, depending on walking speed [6].

That doesn't mean slow walking causes a shorter life. Walking speed reflects many things at once, like heart and lung fitness, muscle strength, balance, and nerve health. That's why doctors sometimes use it as a quick check on how someone is aging.

Does picking up the pace help?

The evidence here is more mixed. Step rate, or cadence, is how many steps you take per minute.

In the large pooled analysis, people whose fastest 30 minutes of the day had a higher step rate still had a lower risk of death after accounting for total steps [2]. The 2023 review also found extra benefit from a moderate or high step rate compared with a low one [3].

But in the study of older women, faster stepping was not clearly linked to lower risk once total steps were taken into account [5]. So total steps seem to matter most, and a brisk pace may add a bit on top.

If you want a simple target for "brisk," a lab study of adults aged 21 to 40 found that about 100 steps per minute lines up with moderate-intensity walking [7]. That study only tested younger adults, so treat it as a rough guide rather than a rule.

Can walking protect your brain as you age?

This is one of the most hopeful areas of walking research, though most of it is still observational.

Steps and dementia risk

A UK study followed 78,430 adults aged 40 to 79 for about 7 years [8]. The lowest risk showed up at about 9,800 steps a day, which was linked to a 51% lower risk of developing dementia [8]. Even about 3,800 steps a day was linked to a 25% lower risk [8].

Pace seemed to matter too. Steps taken at a brisker pace had stronger links to lower risk, and the best results showed up when someone's fastest 30 minutes of the day averaged about 112 steps per minute [8].

The 2025 review combined two studies on dementia and found that 7,000 steps a day was linked to a 38% lower risk, compared with 2,000 steps [1]. The U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines also list a lower risk of dementia, including Alzheimer's disease, among the long-term benefits of regular activity [4].

What happens in the brain

Trials give us a clue about why. In a randomized trial of 120 older adults, aerobic exercise training increased the size of the hippocampus, a part of the brain involved in memory, by 2% [9]. That roughly reversed one to two years of normal age-related shrinkage, and people's spatial memory improved [9]. In the comparison group, the hippocampus kept shrinking [9].

Walking isn't a guaranteed shield against dementia, and many other factors play a role. But staying active is one of the few things linked to brain health that you can start today. For more on mood and thinking, see our guide to walking and your mind.

How does walking help you stay strong and independent?

Living longer matters most if those years feel good. For many people, that means being able to get around, carry groceries, and live on their own terms.

Falls

Falls are a big threat to independence. About 1 in 4 adults aged 65 and older report falling every year, and falls are the leading cause of injury in that age group [10].

Here's an honest nuance. A major Cochrane review of 108 trials found that exercise programs reduce the rate of falls in older adults by 23% [11]. Programs built on balance and functional exercises cut the rate of falls by 24%, and programs mixing several types, usually balance plus strength, probably did even better [11]. But the reviewers were uncertain whether walking programs on their own prevent falls [11].

In step studies, more daily steps were linked to fewer falls, but the evidence was rated very low certainty [1]. So walking is great for your heart, brain, and stamina, but if falls are a concern, it works best alongside balance and strength work.

Bones and muscles

Brisk walking is a weight-bearing exercise, which means your bones carry your body's weight and have to work harder [12]. The National Institutes of Health notes that exercise can make bone denser and helps prevent falls and fractures [12].

That's why the CDC recommends that adults 65 and older do at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, plus muscle-strengthening activities at least 2 days a week, plus activities that improve balance [13]. Simple balance activities include walking heel-to-toe or standing up from a sitting position [13].

Does sitting all day cancel out your walk?

Long hours of sitting are linked to higher health risks, even for people who exercise. The U.S. guidelines boil it down to one line: move more and sit less [4].

The good news is that enough activity seems to offset much of that risk. A huge analysis of more than 1 million people found that the least active people who sat more than 8 hours a day had a 59% higher risk of death during follow-up than the most active people who sat less than 4 hours [14].

But among the most active people, those who sat a lot did not have a higher risk of death [14]. The researchers estimated that about 60 to 75 minutes a day of moderate activity seemed to eliminate the extra risk linked to sitting [14]. That amount didn't fully cancel out the risk linked to lots of TV time, though [14].

You don't need to hit 75 minutes to benefit. Breaking up long sitting stretches with short walks is an easy place to start, and since 2018 the U.S. guidelines no longer require activity to come in 10-minute chunks to count [4].

How to build a walking habit that lasts

The research on longevity is about years of walking, not a burst of motivation in January. That makes habit the real goal. Here's a plan that works at any age:

  1. Find your baseline. Check your phone or watch for your average daily steps over the past week. That's your starting point, not a grade.
  2. Add about 1,000 steps. A short walk around the block is a good start. Hold it for a week or two, then add another 1,000. The biggest gains come early [2][3].
  3. Pick a realistic target. For many people, 7,000 to 8,000 steps a day is a strong goal [1][2]. If you're under 60 and already active, aiming higher is fine [2].
  4. Tie your walk to a cue. Habits form when you repeat a behavior in the same context [15]. Walk after a meal you always eat, after your morning coffee, or when you finish work.
  5. Expect it to take a while. In one well-known study, new daily habits took an average of about 66 days to become automatic [15]. Missing a single day didn't derail the process [15].
  6. Add some pace. Once walking feels routine, add a brisk stretch to your walk. Higher step rates were linked to extra benefit in some studies [2][3].
  7. Add balance and strength. Especially after 65, pair walking with balance moves and muscle-strengthening activities a couple of days a week [13].
  8. Track it. A step counter or walking app turns invisible progress into numbers and streaks. Seeing your trend makes it easier to keep going.

If you can't reach the full guidelines because of a health condition, the CDC's advice is simple: be as physically active as your abilities and conditions allow, because some activity is better than none [13]. For the basics on getting started, see our walking basics guide.

When should you talk to your doctor first?

Walking is safe for most people. Still, check with your doctor before you significantly increase your activity if any of these apply:

  • You have heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, or another long-term health condition.
  • You've had a fall in the past year, feel unsteady on your feet, or use a cane or walker.
  • You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.
  • You have joint pain, a recent injury, or osteoporosis.
  • You're pregnant or recently gave birth.

Your doctor or a physical therapist can help you set a safe starting point, suggest balance exercises, and check whether any medicines affect your balance. Don't change or stop any medicine on your own. If you take medication for a health condition, keep taking it as prescribed and bring any questions to your doctor.

FAQ

Is it ever too late to start walking?

The research suggests no. Older adults saw lower risk with more steps, and the curve flattened at a lower step count for adults 60 and older [2]. Even small increases from a low starting point were linked to benefits [2][3].

Can you walk too much?

In these studies, the benefits for lifespan leveled off at higher step counts rather than reversing [1][5]. Pain or injury is a sign to slow down and build up more gradually.

Do steps from chores and errands count?

Yes. Step studies count all the steps a device records, not just workouts [1]. U.S. guidelines say any amount of activity has some health benefits [4].

Is walking enough exercise as you age?

Walking covers aerobic activity, but adults 65 and older also need muscle-strengthening and balance activities [13]. Those help most with preventing falls [11].
