Do you need special walking shoes?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 23, 2026 6 min read

The short answer

No. For everyday walking, the NHS says any shoes or trainers that are comfortable, supportive, and don't cause blisters will do. Fit matters more than the label: studies suggest 63% to 72% of people wear shoes that don't match the length or width of their feet, and poor fit is linked to foot pain. Choose a pair that fits well and feels good, and save specialist walking shoes for long or rough routes.

You don't need a pricey pair with "walking" printed on the box to start walking. You probably already own something that works.

What matters is fit and comfort. Shoe science has spent decades chasing features like extra cushioning and arch control, and the research is surprisingly humbling about them. Getting the right size, on the other hand, is something lots of us get wrong.

Here's what the evidence says, how to tell if your current shoes are good enough, and what to look for if you do buy a new pair. (New to walking? Start with our walking basics guide.)

Do you really need walking shoes?

For everyday walking, no. The NHS says any shoes or trainers that are comfortable, give enough support, and don't cause blisters will do. The old NIDDK walking guide from the NIH put it the same way: walking doesn't need special equipment, clothing, or training.

So your running shoes, your gym trainers, or your everyday sneakers are all fair game for a commute, a lunch walk, or a lap around the block.

The NHS does suggest that if you start walking longer distances regularly, you might want specialist walking shoes for more challenging routes, plus a waterproof jacket. Think hiking trails, not the walk to the coffee shop.

Does the type of shoe prevent injuries?

Less than the marketing suggests. Most of this research is on runners, not walkers, but it's the best evidence we have on shoe features.

A 2022 Cochrane review pooled 12 trials with 11,240 people. It found that most evidence shows no reduction in lower-limb injuries when comparing different types of running shoes, such as cushioned versus minimalist, or soft versus hard midsoles. Most of that evidence was low or very low certainty.

The same review looked at shoes picked to match your foot type, like "flat feet" or "high arches." In three trials with over 7,000 military recruits, choosing shoes by foot posture probably made little or no difference to injuries, and the results were similar for women and men.

A well-known review from shoe researchers came to a similar place. It found a lack of conclusive evidence that impact forces or ankle rolling (pronation), once thought to be the main predictors of injury, actually explain running injuries. The authors proposed a "comfort filter" instead: people tend to pick shoes that feel comfortable, and that may help keep injury risk down on its own. That's an idea, not a proven rule, but it's a practical one.

Why does fit matter so much?

Because a lot of us are wearing the wrong size. A review of 18 studies found that 63% to 72% of people were wearing shoes that didn't fit the width or length of their feet.

Poorly fitting shoes were linked to foot pain and foot problems like toe deformities, corns, and calluses. In some of the included studies, women were more likely than men to wear shoes that didn't match their feet, including shoes that were too narrow. These were mostly snapshot studies, so they show a link, not proof that bad fit causes the pain.

The fix is simple, and it costs nothing: check the fit before you check the features.

What about walking to work in heels or flats?

If your commute ends at a desk that expects dressier shoes, consider walking in sneakers and changing when you arrive. The NHS suggests exactly that for people who walk to work.

High heels are the main reason why. A review of 18 studies found that high heels were linked to bunions, musculoskeletal pain, and injuries to the person wearing them. Seven of the eight studies on injuries found a real injury toll.

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) says higher-heeled, pointed shoes can cause bunions, knee pain, and low back pain. Its ideal everyday women's shoe has a square or wide toe box and a heel lower than 2 inches. If you love heels, it suggests ones with a platform under the toes to ease the pressure on your forefoot.

Should you try minimalist or barefoot-style shoes?

They're an option, not a requirement. In one trial, 57 runners were randomly assigned to walk in minimalist shoes, do foot-strengthening exercises, or do neither. After 8 weeks, the minimalist-shoe walkers and the exercise group both gained foot muscle size and strength, while the control group didn't change.

The walkers built up gradually, adding more daily steps in the shoes each week. This was a small study in runners, so it doesn't show that minimalist shoes are better for everyone. If you want to try a pair, ease in slowly rather than switching overnight.

Did pregnancy change your shoe size?

It might have. In a study of 49 women, arch height dropped and feet got longer between early pregnancy and about 19 weeks after birth. The changes seemed to last, and the first pregnancy accounted for most of them.

If your shoes started feeling tight or rubbing after having a baby, that could be why, so it's worth getting your feet measured again. Stroller walks are a great way to get moving postpartum, but check with your doctor or midwife before starting exercise during or after pregnancy.

Try this: a 5-minute shoe fit check

Whether you're testing a pair you own or shopping for a new one, run through this list. The tips come from the AAOS unless noted.

  1. Shop late in the day. Your feet can swell up to 8% during the day, so try shoes on toward the evening.
  2. Measure standing up. Your weight spreads your feet. Feet can change size over time, so the AAOS suggests measuring them every year.
  3. Fit the bigger foot. One foot may be a little larger than the other. Buy for that one.
  4. Leave room at the front. You want about 1/2 inch between your longest toe (not always your big toe) and the tip of the shoe.
  5. Wiggle your toes. The toe box should give your toes room to move and never feel cramped. If it's tight, try a wider width or brand instead of a bigger size.
  6. Check the heel. Your heel shouldn't slip out as you walk.
  7. Bring your socks. Try shoes on with the same thickness of sock you'll actually walk in.
  8. Walk, don't just stand. Lace both shoes up and walk around on different surfaces.
  9. Skip shoes that need fixing. Don't buy a shoe that needs stretching or pads to stay on.

If you're shopping, the NIDDK suggests looking for good arch support, a firm and well-cushioned heel, and nonskid, flexible soles. And once you've found a pair, keep your posture relaxed too. Our guide on how to walk with good posture covers that.

When should you talk to a doctor first?

Talk to your doctor, a podiatrist, or a physical therapist about shoes if you have ongoing foot, ankle, knee, or back pain, a past foot injury, or a condition like diabetes that affects your feet. They can tell you whether you need a specific type of shoe or insert.

The same goes if you're pregnant or recently gave birth and your feet hurt or have changed a lot. For everyone else, comfortable shoes that fit are enough to get going. If getting going is the hard part, try our tips on how to start walking when you hate exercise.

FAQ

Can I walk in running shoes?

Yes. The NHS says any comfortable, supportive trainers that don't cause blisters are fine for walking.

How often should I replace my shoes?

There's no proven number for walking shoes. For running shoes, the AAOS says cushioning has lost 45% of its shock absorption by 500 miles. Replace walking shoes when they feel flat or worn.

Are expensive shoes better for walking?

Not necessarily. In runners, research hasn't shown that fancier shoe features prevent injuries. Fit and comfort matter more than price.

Does foot type matter when choosing shoes?

Less than you'd think. Picking shoes by foot posture probably made little or no difference to injuries in a large Cochrane review.

    Basics · 6 min readThere's no single best time. Large studies disagree, linking morning, midday, and evening activity to lower risks, and walking at any time of day is linked to a longer life. If you want a tiebreaker, a 10-minute walk after each meal lowered after-meal blood sugar about 12% more than one daily 30-minute walk in a trial of adults with type 2 diabetes.

  • What is a good daily step goal?

    Basics · 6 min readThere's no single magic number. Large studies link about 7,000 steps a day to much lower health risks than 2,000, with benefits leveling off around 6,000 to 8,000 steps for adults 60 and older and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults. The best personal goal starts from your current daily average and climbs a little at a time.
