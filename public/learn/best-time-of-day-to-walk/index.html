What's the best time of day to walk?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 13, 2026 6 min read

The short answer

There's no single best time. Large studies disagree, linking morning, midday, and evening activity to lower risks, and walking at any time of day is linked to a longer life. If you want a tiebreaker, a 10-minute walk after each meal lowered after-meal blood sugar about 12% more than one daily 30-minute walk in a trial of adults with type 2 diabetes.

Morning people swear by sunrise walks. Night owls say an evening stroll is the only way to unwind. And the internet has a strong opinion for every hour in between.

Researchers have started looking at this too, using activity trackers on tens of thousands of people. The results are interesting, but they don't agree with each other. Here's what they found, where the timing really does seem to matter, and how to pick a time that works for you. (New to walking? Start with our walking basics guide.)

Does the time of day you walk actually matter?

Less than how much you walk. The biggest study on this, which tracked 92,139 adults with wrist accelerometers for a median of more than 7 years, found that moderate-to-vigorous activity at any time of day was linked to lower risks of death from any cause, heart disease, and cancer [1].

The CDC's advice reflects the same idea. Adults need at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, such as brisk walking, and you can spread it across the week in smaller chunks [2]. Its guidance doesn't name a best hour. It says some physical activity is better than none [2].

So if the only time you can walk is 9:45 p.m. after the kids are asleep, that walk still counts.

Is morning or evening walking better?

The honest answer is that the big studies point in different directions. All three below used the same group of UK adults (the UK Biobank) and the same kind of tracker, and they still landed in different places.

The case for midday

In the 92,139-person study, people who did more than half of their daily moderate-to-vigorous activity in one time window were grouped as morning (5 a.m. to 11 a.m.), midday-afternoon (11 a.m. to 5 p.m.), or evening (5 p.m. to midnight) [1]. Compared with the morning group, the midday-afternoon and mixed-timing groups had lower risks of death from any cause and from heart disease [1]. The evening group didn't [1].

The case for late morning

A second study of 86,657 adults grouped people by their daily activity pattern, regardless of how active they were overall [3]. People who tended to be most active in the late morning had a lower risk of coronary artery disease (a hazard ratio of 0.84) and stroke (0.83) than people with a midday pattern [3]. The link was stronger in women [3].

The case for evening

A third study looked only at 29,836 adults with higher body weight (a BMI of 30 or more) [4]. Compared with people who rarely did any activity bouts, those who did most of their brisk activity in the evening had the lowest risk of death (a hazard ratio of 0.39) [4]. Afternoon (0.60) and morning (0.67) activity were also linked to lower risk, just less so [4]. Results were similar in people who also had type 2 diabetes [4].

What to make of it

These are observational studies. They show links, not cause and effect. People who walk at a certain time may differ in other ways, like their jobs, sleep, or health, that the researchers couldn't fully account for.

When three large studies of similar people point to three different "best" times, the safest takeaway is that no time of day has been proven best. Every timing group that was active did better than being inactive [1][4].

Is walking after meals a good idea?

This is where timing has the strongest evidence, and it comes from trials, not just observation.

In a randomized crossover trial, 41 adults with type 2 diabetes tried two plans for two weeks each [5]. In one, they walked 30 minutes a day at any time. In the other, they walked 10 minutes after each main meal [5]. The total walking time was the same.

Blood sugar in the three hours after meals was about 12% lower with the after-meal walks [5]. The biggest drop, about 22%, came after the evening meal, when people ate the most carbohydrate and sat the most [5]. The authors suggested guidelines should specify walking after meals, especially carb-heavy ones [5].

This isn't just for people with diabetes. A meta-analysis of seven short trials, mostly in adults with higher body weight, compared long stretches of sitting with frequent short breaks [6]. Light walking breaks lowered blood sugar and insulin after meals compared with sitting the whole time [6]. They also worked better than standing breaks [6].

It's a small habit with a real effect. For more on blood sugar and weight, see our guide to walking for weight loss.

Will walking in the evening mess up your sleep?

Probably not. A common worry is that exercise late in the day keeps you awake. A review of 23 studies in healthy adults tested that idea [7].

Overall, evening exercise didn't hurt sleep [7]. If anything, people got slightly more deep sleep (slow-wave sleep went up 1.3 percentage points) [7]. The authors concluded the studies "do not support the hypothesis that evening exercise negatively affects sleep" [7].

The one exception was vigorous exercise ending an hour or less before bed, which might make it harder to fall asleep and cut total sleep time [7]. A relaxed walk after dinner isn't that. If you're doing hard intervals, finish them a bit earlier.

The CDC also notes that physical activity in general can help you sleep better [8].

What about walking in the heat?

On hot days, the clock matters for safety. The CDC advises limiting outdoor activity in the middle of the day, when the sun is hottest, and scheduling workouts earlier or later in the day when it's cooler [9].

It also suggests starting slow and picking up the pace gradually, drinking more water than usual before you feel thirsty, and wearing loose, light-colored clothing [9]. If you feel faint or weak, stop and get somewhere cool [9].

In summer, that may decide the question for you: early morning or evening.

How to find your best walking time

The best time is the one you'll actually repeat. Try this:

  1. Look for a gap that already exists. Before work, a lunch break, or after dinner. Pick the slot that's free most days.
  2. Attach it to something you already do. "After I clear the dinner plates, I walk." A cue makes it automatic. Our guide on how to start walking when you hate exercise has more on this.
  3. Add a short walk after your biggest meal. Even 10 minutes helps after-meal blood sugar [5][6].
  4. Split it up if you need to. Three 10-minute walks count toward your weekly minutes [2].
  5. Check the forecast in summer. Move your walk away from the hottest hours [9].
  6. Keep hard workouts away from bedtime. An easy walk at night is fine. Save the sprints for earlier [7].
  7. Track it for a couple of weeks. A step counter or walking app shows which time you actually stick with, not just the one you planned.

Once you have a routine, aim to build toward 150 minutes a week [2]. For how many steps that adds up to, see how many steps a day to live longer.

Talk to your doctor first if...

If you have type 2 diabetes, heart disease, or high blood pressure, check with your doctor before changing your walking routine. That's especially true if you take insulin or other medicine that lowers blood sugar, since adding activity after meals can change your readings. Don't adjust any medication on your own.

The same goes if you're pregnant, recovering from an injury, or have a higher risk of falls. Walking in the dark or in the heat brings its own risks, so your doctor can help you choose a safe time and place.

FAQ

Is it better to walk before or after eating?

For blood sugar, after. A 10-minute walk after each main meal lowered after-meal blood sugar more than one daily walk did in a trial [5].

Does walking at night help you lose weight?

There's no strong evidence that one time of day burns more for weight loss. Total activity matters more, and walking at any time is linked to health benefits [1][2].

Is it bad to walk right before bed?

A gentle walk is unlikely to hurt your sleep. Only vigorous exercise ending within about an hour of bedtime seemed to disrupt sleep in one review [7].

Should I walk in the morning on an empty stomach?

The studies here don't answer that directly. If you feel shaky or have diabetes, eat first or ask your doctor what's safe for you.
