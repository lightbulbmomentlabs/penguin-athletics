Stress is your body's response to something outside you, like a deadline, a fight with a friend, or an illness. A little of it can help you push through. A lot of it, for a long time, can mess with your sleep, your mood, and your body.

Walking won't fix the thing that's stressing you. But it can change how your body and brain handle that stress. Here's what the research says about how it works, how much to walk, and where to do it. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to [walking and your mind](/learn/walking-mental-health/).)

## What does stress do to your body?

The National Institute of Mental Health describes stress as the physical or mental response to an outside cause [1]. It can be a one-time thing or something that keeps happening over a long time [1].

Short bursts of stress can even help, like pushing you to meet a deadline [1]. But when stress and anxiety don't let up, NIMH notes they can affect your sleep and your immune, digestive, heart, and reproductive systems [1]. Long-term stress may also raise your risk of anxiety disorders or depression [1].

That's why having a few reliable ways to bring stress down matters. NIMH lists exercise among the simple things to try when you start to feel overwhelmed [1].

## Does walking actually reduce stress?

The evidence says physical activity helps, and walking is part of that picture.

A large 2023 umbrella review pulled together 97 reviews covering 1,039 trials and 128,119 adults [2]. Physical activity had a medium-sized effect on psychological distress compared with usual care (an effect size of -0.60) [2]. That benefit held across healthy adults, people with mental health conditions, and people with chronic diseases [2]. Most of the reviews it included were rated low quality, so treat the exact number with some caution [2].

Walking-specific research is thinner. A 2018 scoping review of walking and mental health found that the evidence for psychological stress varied in how much there was and how well walking worked [3]. Importantly, it found no harmful effects of walking [3]. So the honest summary is: exercise reliably helps with distress, and walking is the easiest way most people can get it, but more walking-only studies are needed.

If anxiety is the bigger issue for you, our article on [whether walking helps anxiety](/learn/does-walking-help-anxiety/) goes deeper.

## How does walking calm your stress response?

Researchers are still working this out. A few explanations have support.

### A steadier stress hormone rhythm

Cortisol is one of your body's main stress hormones. It normally peaks in the morning and falls through the day. A 2023 meta-analysis found that people who were more physically active tended to have a steeper daily drop in cortisol, which is a sign of healthier regulation [4].

The link was small, though, and the authors found some signs of publication bias [4]. They also found no difference in the morning cortisol spike [4]. So this is a clue, not proof that walking resets your hormones.

### A smaller reaction when stress hits

Some studies test this in a lab by putting people through a stressful task, like giving a speech to judges. A 2018 review of 14 such studies found that about half showed a smaller stress response in people who were more active or fitter [5]. Cortisol rose less in 7 of 12 studies, and heart rate rose less in 4 of 9 [5].

Most of those studies were snapshots in time, not experiments, so they show a link rather than cause and effect [5]. Still, it fits the idea that regular movement may help your body react less sharply to everyday stress.

### A break from replaying the same thoughts

Stress often comes with rumination, the habit of going over the same worries again and again. In one experiment, healthy adults took a 90-minute walk in either a natural setting or a city setting [6]. The nature walk lowered self-reported rumination and activity in a brain area linked to it, while the city walk didn't [6]. It was one study, but it suggests that where you walk may matter for your head, too.

## Is a walk in nature better for stress?

There's decent evidence that green space adds something extra.

In a 2019 study, 36 city dwellers spent time in a nature spot of their choice at least three times a week for 8 weeks [7]. After each nature visit, their salivary cortisol fell 21.3% per hour beyond its normal daily drop [7]. Whether people sat, walked, or did both didn't change the cortisol result [7].

A smaller 2024 study had 17 people walk both a green path and an urban road [8]. Cortisol was lower after both walks, but it dropped more after the green walk (53% on average) than the urban one (37%) [8]. With so few people, it's a hint, not a final answer.

A 2019 review of "forest bathing," the Japanese practice of spending calm time in a forest, looked at 22 studies [9]. In all but two, cortisol was lower after time in the forest compared with a comparison group, or fell from before to after [9]. The authors said the effect is short-term, that expectations may play a big role, and that more research is needed [9].

Bottom line: any walk counts. If you can reach a park, a tree-lined street, or a trail, that may give you a little more stress relief for the same effort.

## How long should you walk to reduce stress?

Shorter than you might think.

In the nature study above, the biggest cortisol drop for the time spent came from 20 to 30 minutes [7]. Benefits kept building after that, just more slowly [7]. That makes 20 to 30 minutes a good target for a stress walk.

For everyday mental health, NIMH says just 30 minutes of walking every day can boost your mood and improve your health [10]. It also points out that small amounts of exercise add up, so you don't have to do all 30 minutes at once [10].

Pace is up to you. In the umbrella review, higher-intensity activity was linked to bigger improvements in symptoms [2]. But on a stressful day, an easy stroll you actually take beats a hard workout you skip.

## How to take a stress-busting walk

Try this the next time stress is building:

1. **Go for 20 to 30 minutes.** That seems to be the sweet spot for stress relief per minute spent [7]. Ten minutes still beats zero.
2. **Pick somewhere green if you can.** A park, a trail, or a street with trees may give you extra benefit [7][8].
3. **Leave the problem at the door.** Don't take a work call. Put your phone in your pocket, or play music you find calming.
4. **Notice your surroundings.** Look for three things you can see and two you can hear. It gives your mind somewhere to go besides your worries.
5. **Walk at a pace that feels good.** Start easy. Pick it up if you want to burn off nervous energy.
6. **Make it a habit.** Schedule it like a meeting, at the same time each day. Regular activity is where the bigger benefits show up [2][4].
7. **Track it.** A step counter or walking app makes small wins easy to see, which helps on the days you don't feel like going.

## When stress needs more than a walk

Walking is a good tool, but it's not a treatment for anxiety disorders or depression. NIMH says to seek professional help if you have severe or distressing symptoms that last two weeks or more, such as trouble sleeping, changes in appetite, or a hard time getting out of bed because of your mood [10].

If you take medication for anxiety, depression, or another condition, keep taking it as prescribed. Don't stop or change it without talking to your doctor. Walking can work alongside the treatment you already have.

If you have heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, or a health condition that makes exercise tricky, talk to your doctor before starting a new walking routine.

If you or someone you know is struggling or thinking about suicide, call or text **988** to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the US. It's free, confidential, and available 24/7 [10]. In a life-threatening emergency, call 911.

## FAQ

### Can one walk lower stress right away?

It can help. In one small study, cortisol was lower after a single walk, and it fell more on a green path [8]. Bigger, lasting benefits come from regular activity [2].

### Is walking or running better for stress?

Both help. Higher-intensity activity was linked to bigger gains in one large review [2], but the best choice is the one you'll stick with.

### Does a treadmill walk still help?

Yes. Physical activity in general reduces distress [2]. Outdoor green walks may add a small extra boost [7][8].

### What's the best time of day for a stress walk?

Whenever you'll do it. There's no strong evidence for one time. A walk at a set time each day is easier to keep up.
