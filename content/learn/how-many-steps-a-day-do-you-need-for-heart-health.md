Step counters made "10,000 steps" feel like the magic number. But when researchers look at heart disease specifically, the picture is friendlier than that.

Large studies that tracked people's steps for years found that heart benefits start well below 10,000 a day. Each extra chunk of steps seems to help most when you're starting from a low number.

Here's what the research shows, where the benefits seem to level off, whether walking faster matters, and how to build up. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to [walking and your heart](/learn/walking-heart-health/).)

## What do step studies show about heart disease?

The clearest evidence comes from studies that gave people a step-counting device, then followed them for years to see who developed heart problems. These are observational studies. They show links, not proof that steps alone cause the difference.

A 2023 meta-analysis in the journal *Circulation* pooled eight of these studies, with more than 20,000 adults followed for about 6 years on average [1]. It counted heart attacks and other coronary heart disease, strokes, and heart failure [1].

In adults 60 and older, taking about **6,000 to 9,000 steps a day** was linked to a **40% to 50% lower risk** of heart disease compared with about 2,000 steps a day [1]. The more steps people took, the lower their risk tended to be [1].

A second 2023 meta-analysis, of 12 studies and more than 111,000 people, found a similar pattern [2]. Compared with 2,000 steps a day, a lower risk of heart disease showed up at just **2,735 steps a day** [2]. Risk kept falling with more steps until about **7,126 steps a day**, where it was about half that of the 2,000-step group [2].

## Where do the benefits level off?

In most studies, the curve is steep at first and then flattens. Going from very few steps to a moderate number is where the biggest drop in risk shows up.

The *Circulation* meta-analysis described a "steep early slope" [1]. Above about 6,000 steps, the curve was not as steep, but higher step counts still seemed to come with a lower risk in older adults [1].

A large 2025 review in *The Lancet Public Health* pulled together 57 studies [3]. For heart disease, the benefits began to level off at around **5,000 to 7,000 steps a day** [3]. Compared with 2,000 steps, **7,000 steps** was linked to a **25% lower risk** of developing heart disease [3].

For dying of heart disease, though, that review found a straight-line pattern: more steps went with lower risk without a clear plateau [3]. Only three studies looked at that, so the researchers rated the evidence as low certainty [3].

Another 2023 meta-analysis of 17 studies found that every extra 500 steps a day was linked to a **7% lower risk** of dying from heart disease [4]. That's a useful reminder that small increases may count.

## Is 10,000 steps a day necessary?

For heart health, the evidence says probably not. The authors of the *Circulation* study note that the 10,000 goal "is not evidence based" and came from a marketing campaign in Japan [1].

Their findings suggest step goals for heart health in older adults could reasonably sit **below 10,000** [1]. The 2025 review reached a similar conclusion. It called 7,000 steps a day a more realistic target for some people, while noting that 10,000 is still a fine goal if you're already more active [3].

So 10,000 isn't wrong. It's just not the cutoff for heart benefits. If you're at 3,000 now, getting to 5,000 or 7,000 is a real win.

## Do younger and older adults need different step counts?

The step studies found the clearest heart benefits in older adults. In the *Circulation* meta-analysis, more steps were linked to lower heart disease risk in people 60 and older, but not in people under 60 [1].

That doesn't mean steps don't matter for younger people. Heart disease often takes years to show up, and the studies may not have followed younger adults long enough to see it [1]. Only 4.2% of younger adults had a heart event during the study, compared with 9.5% of older adults [1].

The researchers suggested that for younger and middle-aged adults, the better thing to measure may be heart disease risk factors, like high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and diabetes [1]. Walking can help with those too. (See [does walking lower blood pressure?](/learn/does-walking-lower-blood-pressure/))

## Does walking pace matter, or just the number of steps?

Here the evidence is mixed. Total steps have the strongest support. Pace may add something extra, but studies don't fully agree.

Researchers often measure pace as **cadence**, or steps per minute. In lab studies, walking at about **100 steps per minute** matched moderate intensity in adults aged 21 to 60 [5][7]. About 130 steps per minute matched vigorous intensity [7].

A UK study of 78,500 adults found that people with a faster average pace during their 30 fastest minutes of the day (not necessarily in a row) had a lower risk of heart disease, even after accounting for their total steps [6]. The researchers said steps at a higher cadence "may be associated with additional risk reduction" [6].

The *Circulation* meta-analysis, on the other hand, found no link between step rate and heart disease once total steps were taken into account [1]. And the 2023 meta-analysis of 12 studies found that a faster pace was linked to a lower risk of death from any cause [2].

Bottom line: get the steps first. If you can, make some of them brisk.

## How do steps relate to 150 minutes a week?

The CDC recommends adults get **150 minutes a week** of moderate activity, such as brisk walking, plus muscle-strengthening activity on 2 or more days [8]. One way to get there is 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week [8].

Steps and minutes can work together. At about 100 steps per minute, a brisk 30-minute walk adds up to about **3,000 steps** [5]. Experts note those steps need to be on top of your normal daily movement [5].

When researchers combined everyday movement with the recommended amount of brisk activity, they estimated it works out to roughly **7,000 to 8,000 steps a day** [5]. That lines up closely with where heart benefits start to level off in the newer studies [2][3].

You don't need a step counter to know what brisk feels like. The CDC lists walking at **2.5 miles per hour or faster** as moderate intensity [9]. At that pace, you can talk but not sing [9].

## How to build up your steps

The goal is steady progress, not a big jump on day one. Try this:

1. **Find your starting number.** Check your phone or step counter for a typical week and take the average. That's your baseline.
2. **Add 1,000 to 2,000 steps.** Research on step programs shows people typically increase by about 2,000 to 2,500 steps a day [5]. At about 100 steps per minute, a 10- to 20-minute walk covers that [5].
3. **Hold it for a week or two.** Once it feels normal, add another 1,000.
4. **Aim for 7,000 to 9,000.** That's the range where heart benefits seem to level off in most studies [1][2][3]. Go higher if you enjoy it.
5. **Make one walk brisk.** A 30-minute walk where you can talk but not sing covers a big chunk of your weekly 150 minutes [8][9].
6. **Split it up.** Short walks after meals, a lap during calls, or parking farther away all count. The CDC notes you can break activity into smaller chunks [8].
7. **Watch the trend, not the day.** Some days will be low. Weekly averages tell the real story.

## When should you talk to your doctor?

Walking is safe for most people, but check with your doctor before ramping up if any of these apply:

- You have heart disease, or you've had a heart attack or stroke.
- You have high blood pressure, diabetes, or another long-term condition.
- You take heart or blood pressure medicine.
- You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.
- You have joint problems or a higher risk of falls.

If you take medicine, keep taking it as prescribed. More steps work alongside treatment, not instead of it.

## FAQ

### Is 5,000 steps a day enough for your heart?

It's a solid start. In one large review, heart disease benefits began to level off at around 5,000 to 7,000 steps a day [3]. More may still help [1].

### Can you walk too many steps for your heart?

In these studies, benefits tended to level off at higher step counts rather than reverse [1][3]. One analysis that went up to 20,000 steps a day described it as "the more the better" [4].

### Do steps from daily chores count?

Yes. Step studies counted all steps measured by a device, not just workouts [1][3]. Brisk walks may add extra benefit [6].

### What is a brisk walking pace in steps?

About 100 steps per minute or more is a common guide for moderate intensity in adults [7].
