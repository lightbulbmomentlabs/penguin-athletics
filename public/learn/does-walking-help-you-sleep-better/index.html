Does walking help you sleep better?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 19, 2026 6 min read

The short answer

Yes, for many people it does. A 2026 review of 21 trials found that regular walking improved self-reported sleep quality in adults, with the biggest gains in people who slept worst to start with. The certainty is still low, and walking works best alongside proven insomnia treatment, not instead of it.

If you've ever stared at the ceiling at 2 a.m., you've probably been told to "get more exercise." It sounds too simple. But walking is one of the most studied, lowest-effort habits linked to better sleep.

It won't fix every sleep problem, and it isn't a treatment for insomnia on its own. Here's what the research says, how much walking it takes, and whether an evening walk will keep you up. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to walking and your mind.)

What does the research say about walking and sleep?

The most direct evidence comes from reviews that looked only at walking.

A 2026 meta-analysis pooled 21 randomized trials with 1,707 adults. Walking produced a medium-to-large improvement in how well people said they slept, compared with people who didn't walk or did non-aerobic activities. People who slept worst at the start tended to improve the most. The big caveat: the authors rated the certainty of the evidence as very low.

Another 2026 review focused on middle-aged and older adults and included 8 trials with 683 people. Walking was linked to about 34 more minutes of total sleep and about 12 fewer minutes awake during the night. It didn't clearly improve how quickly people fell asleep, and the authors said the evidence should be read with caution.

What about exercise in general?

The wider exercise research points the same way. The U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines say there is strong evidence that moderate-to-vigorous activity, such as brisk walking, improves sleep quality in adults. It helps people fall asleep faster and spend less time awake at night, and it can increase deep sleep and reduce daytime sleepiness.

A meta-analysis of 66 studies found that regular exercise had small benefits for total sleep time and sleep efficiency, and moderate benefits for sleep quality.

Does walking help with insomnia?

It may. A 2026 network meta-analysis compared exercise types in 22 trials of 1,348 adults with insomnia. Walking or jogging may lead to a large drop in insomnia severity scores, about 9.6 points on a standard insomnia questionnaire. Yoga and tai chi also did well, and the authors named all three as more effective than other exercises.

That evidence was rated low certainty, and most of the trials had some risk of bias. So it's promising, not proven.

Walking also isn't the first-line fix for long-term insomnia. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute says cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is usually recommended as the first treatment option. Walking can sit alongside it.

Who seems to benefit?

Walking has been tested in a range of groups:

  • Older adults. In a review of 35 trials with 3,519 older adults, several exercise programs improved sleep quality compared with usual care. The top performer was muscle endurance training combined with walking. Walking alone ranked lower, which suggests adding some strength work may help.
  • People with cancer. A review of 25 trials with 1,918 people found walking improved sleep disturbance more than yoga did. Most participants had breast cancer.
  • Women after menopause. In a 12-week trial of 112 women, those who walked with a pedometer and added 500 steps a week improved their sleep quality scores more than women who didn't.

Do more daily steps mean better sleep?

There's a link, but it's modest. In a study of 855 older adults in Japan who wore a sensor for months, more daily steps were tied to better sleep efficiency and less time awake at night. That study was observational, so it shows a link, not cause and effect.

A smaller study of 59 adults tracked steps and sleep for four weeks. On days when people were more active than usual, they reported better sleep quality and duration. Across the whole month, the link with sleep quality was stronger in women than in men.

Will an evening walk keep you awake?

Probably not. A review of 23 studies in healthy adults found that evening exercise didn't hurt sleep overall, and people got slightly more deep sleep. The one exception was vigorous exercise ending an hour or less before bed, which might make it harder to fall asleep.

The Physical Activity Guidelines also found similar sleep benefits whether activity happened more than 8 hours before bed, 3 to 8 hours before, or less than 3 hours before.

Advice varies here, though. NHLBI's sleep tips suggest daytime activity at least 5 to 6 hours before bed, noting that exercising close to bedtime can make it harder to fall asleep. A relaxed walk after dinner is very different from hard intervals at 10 p.m. If late walks seem to wind you up, move them earlier. For more on timing, see the best time of day to walk.

How much walking helps you sleep?

You don't need a marathon. Based on the trials, the 2026 review suggested moderate-intensity walking three to five times a week, 30 to 60 minutes a session, for 12 to 24 weeks. The review of middle-aged and older adults found that sessions of 30 minutes or more may bring bigger benefits.

That lines up with the Physical Activity Guidelines' target of at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week. And you don't have to wait months to notice something. A single session of moderate-to-vigorous activity can improve sleep that same day.

Most adults aged 18 to 60 need 7 or more hours of sleep a night, according to the CDC. Walking can help, but it can't replace the hours themselves.

How to start walking for better sleep

  1. Start with what you can do. Even 10 to 15 minutes counts. Build toward 30 minutes most days.
  2. Pick a regular time. A walk at the same time each day is easier to keep, and NHLBI recommends a consistent sleep and wake time too.
  3. Go brisk, not brutal. The 2026 review suggested moderate-intensity walking. If you do hard workouts, finish them more than an hour before bed.
  4. Add a little strength work. Pairing walking with muscle endurance training worked best for sleep in older adults.
  5. Count your steps. Adding about 500 steps a week was enough to help in one trial. A step counter makes that easy to track.
  6. Give it a few weeks. Many of the trials ran for 12 weeks or more. Notice how you sleep, not just how far you walk.

When to talk to your doctor

Talk to your doctor if poor sleep keeps getting in the way of your days. Long-term insomnia has proven treatments, like CBT-I, that a walk can't replace.

Check in first before starting a new walking routine if you have heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, an injury, or a higher risk of falls, or if you're pregnant. If you take sleep medicine, keep taking it as prescribed. Don't stop or change it without talking to your doctor.

FAQ

How long should I walk to sleep better?

Trials that helped sleep often used 30 to 60 minutes, three to five times a week. Sessions of 30 minutes or more may work better.

Is it bad to walk right before bed?

A gentle walk is unlikely to hurt your sleep. Only vigorous exercise ending within about an hour of bedtime seemed to disrupt sleep in one review.

Can one walk help me sleep tonight?

It might. A single session of moderate-to-vigorous activity can improve sleep that same day, and the benefits grow with regular activity.

Is walking better than yoga for sleep?

It depends on the group. In people with insomnia, yoga, tai chi, and walking or jogging all did well. Choose the one you'll keep doing.

  • Do you need special walking shoes?

    Basics · 6 min readNo. For everyday walking, the NHS says any shoes or trainers that are comfortable, supportive, and don't cause blisters will do. Fit matters more than the label: studies suggest 63% to 72% of people wear shoes that don't match the length or width of their feet, and poor fit is linked to foot pain. Choose a pair that fits well and feels good, and save specialist walking shoes for long or rough routes.
