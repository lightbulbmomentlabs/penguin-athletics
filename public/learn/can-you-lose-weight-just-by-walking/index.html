Walking for weight loss

Can you lose weight just by walking?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 6, 2026 6 min read

The short answer

Yes, but usually only a little. In trials of walking alone, people lost about 1.4 kg (roughly 3 pounds) on average, and programs that combine walking with eating changes lead to more weight loss. Walking still improves blood pressure, blood sugar, and fitness even when the scale barely moves.

Walking is the most popular way people try to get more active, so it's natural to wonder whether it can change your weight on its own. The honest answer is that it can, but the change is usually small.

That's not the whole story, though. Walking does a lot for your body that a scale can't measure. Here's what the research shows, how much walking the studies used, and how to build a plan you can stick with. (For the full picture, see our guide to walking for weight loss.)

How much weight do people lose from walking alone?

The best evidence comes from reviews that pool many randomized trials. One review looked at 32 reports of trials where inactive adults walked and changed nothing else, compared with people who didn't exercise [1]. On average, the walkers lost about 1.37 kg (roughly 3 pounds) [1].

A second review looked only at pedometer-based walking programs with no diet changes [2]. Across 9 studies and 307 people, the average weight loss was 1.27 kg [2]. That worked out to about 0.05 kg a week (about a tenth of a pound) [2].

Those numbers are real, but they're modest. A review of exercise and weight came to the same conclusion. Aerobic exercise at public health levels may lead to "up to modest weight loss (~2 kg)," and results vary a lot from person to person [3].

Why isn't the effect bigger?

The CDC puts it simply: "Most weight loss occurs from decreasing calories" [6]. Walking does use energy, but the amount from a typical walking program is small compared with what you eat in a day. That's why walking alone tends to move the scale slowly.

Does walking longer help?

Yes. In the pedometer review, longer programs led to more weight loss than shorter ones [2]. The programs ran from 4 weeks to 1 year, with a median of 16 weeks [2]. So if walking is your main tool, patience matters.

What happens when you combine walking with eating changes?

This is where the bigger results show up. A review of trials lasting at least 12 months compared programs that combined diet and physical activity with programs that used physical activity alone [4].

The combined programs led to about 6.29 kg more weight loss at 12 to 18 months than activity alone [4]. Compared with diet-only programs, the combined approach was about the same at 3 to 6 months. By 12 months, though, the combined programs led to about 1.72 kg more weight loss [4].

In other words, eating changes tend to do most of the early work. Activity seems to help the results last. The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) reached a similar view: moderate activity improved weight loss in studies that used moderate diet changes [5].

We're not going to tell you what to eat here. If you want to change your eating, a doctor or registered dietitian can help you find an approach that's safe and realistic for you.

Can walking help you keep weight off?

This is one of walking's most useful jobs, though the evidence is less certain than for weight loss.

The CDC says regular physical activity is needed to keep weight off. It also notes that you "may need more than the recommended minutes" of activity a week to maintain your weight [6]. The ACSM found that people who kept weight off tended to do more than 250 minutes a week of activity [5].

There's an important catch. That evidence comes mostly from observational studies, not controlled trials [5]. A large 2021 overview of exercise reviews also found no significant effect of exercise on weight maintenance in the one review that measured it [8]. So activity is clearly linked to keeping weight off, but researchers are still working out how much it causes on its own.

Walking may also help prevent weight gain in the first place. A review for the U.S. physical activity guidelines found strong evidence linking physical activity with less weight gain in adults [7]. The effect was clearest above 150 minutes a week of moderate-to-vigorous activity [7].

What does walking do even if the scale doesn't move?

This is the part that often gets lost. Many of walking's biggest benefits show up whether or not you lose weight. Researchers who study exercise and weight urge doctors to stress that "numerous health benefits occur" from activity "in the absence of weight loss" [3]. The ACSM also notes that activity improves health risk even without weight loss [5].

Here's what walking trials have found:

  • Blood pressure: In walking-only trials, systolic pressure (the top number) fell by about 3.58 mmHg [1]. Read more in does walking lower blood pressure?
  • Blood sugar: In people with type 2 diabetes, walking lowered HbA1c, a measure of average blood sugar, by 0.50% [9]. Supervised walking, or walking with motivational support, worked best [9].
  • Fitness: Walking raised aerobic capacity, a key measure of heart and lung fitness [1].
  • Waist and body fat: Walking trimmed waist size by about 1.51 cm and body fat by about 1.22 percentage points [1]. These can change a little even when weight changes only slightly.

There's also a good reason not to dismiss small weight changes. The ACSM notes that losing 3% to 5% of body weight is linked to a lower health risk [5].

How much walking did the studies use?

The amount matters, so here's what the evidence points to:

  • 150 minutes a week is the CDC's baseline for overall health. That's about 30 minutes, five days a week, at a brisk pace [6].
  • 150 to 250 minutes a week of moderate activity gave "only modest weight loss" in the ACSM review [5].
  • More than 250 minutes a week was linked to clinically meaningful weight loss [5].

If you track steps instead of minutes, that works too. In a review of pedometer studies, people using pedometers walked about 2,491 more steps a day than people who didn't [10]. Having a step goal was one of the strongest predictors of walking more [10].

"Brisk" means a pace that speeds up your breathing and heart rate. The CDC lists brisk walking as moderate-intensity activity [6].

A realistic plan for walking and weight

Walking won't transform the scale overnight. It can become a steady habit that supports your health for years. Try this:

  1. Start small. If you're not active now, begin with short walks you can manage most days. Our guide to starting when you hate exercise has more ideas.
  2. Build toward 150 minutes a week. Add a few minutes each week until you reach about 30 minutes, five days a week [6].
  3. Then keep going if you can. Moving toward 250 minutes a week lines up with the amounts linked to more weight loss and keeping weight off [5].
  4. Set a step goal. Step goals helped people walk more in the research [10]. Pick a number a bit above your current average and nudge it up.
  5. Give it months, not days. Weight change from walking builds slowly, and longer programs saw more results [2].
  6. Track more than weight. Notice your energy, your sleep, how far you can walk, and your blood pressure or blood sugar if you check them.
  7. Pair it with support. If weight loss is your goal, talk with a doctor or dietitian about eating changes too [4].

When should you talk to your doctor?

Walking is safe for most people. Check with your doctor first if any of these apply:

  • You have heart disease, high blood pressure, or diabetes.
  • You take medicine that affects your blood sugar or blood pressure.
  • You have joint pain, an injury, or a higher risk of falls.
  • You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.
  • You have a history of an eating disorder, or you find that focusing on weight affects your mental health.

Your doctor can help you set goals that fit your health, and they don't have to be about weight at all.

FAQ

How long until walking changes my weight?

Slowly. In pedometer programs, people lost about 0.05 kg a week on average, and longer programs led to more weight loss [2].

Is 10,000 steps a day enough to lose weight?

There's no magic number. Having any step goal, such as 10,000 steps, helped people walk more [10]. But walking alone usually brings modest weight loss [1].

Is walking better than diet for weight loss?

Eating changes usually drive more of the early weight loss [6]. Programs that combine eating changes and activity tend to do better than activity alone [4].

Why am I walking but not losing weight?

That's common. Walking alone often moves the scale only a little [3]. You may still be improving your blood pressure, fitness, and blood sugar [1][9].

  • Basics · 7 min read

    How to start walking when you hate exercise

    Start smaller than you think you need to, like a 5 to 10 minute walk tied to something you already do every day, and add a few minutes each week. Health guidelines say some activity is better than none and short walks count. New habits took about 66 days on average to feel automatic in one study, so aim for consistency, not intensity.
  • Longevity · 7 min read

    How many steps a day to live longer?

    In a pooled analysis of 15 studies, the risk of dying early kept falling as steps rose, then leveled off at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for adults 60 and older and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults. You don't need 10,000 to benefit: a 2025 review linked 7,000 steps a day to a 47% lower risk of death than 2,000 steps.
  • Heart · 6 min read

    How many steps a day do you need for heart health?

    You probably don't need 10,000. In large studies, people who took more steps had a lower risk of heart disease, with most of the benefit showing up by about 7,000 to 9,000 steps a day. In adults 60 and older, 6,000 to 9,000 steps a day was linked to a 40% to 50% lower risk of heart disease than about 2,000 steps.
  • Heart · 6 min read

    Does walking lower blood pressure?

    Yes. A large Cochrane review of 73 trials found that regular walking lowered systolic blood pressure (the top number) by about 4 mmHg on average. Most of the walking programs in those trials averaged around 150 minutes of moderate walking a week for a few months.
