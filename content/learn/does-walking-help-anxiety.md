When your mind is racing, "go for a walk" can sound like advice from someone who has never felt anxious. But there's real research behind it. Walking is one of the most studied, lowest-effort ways to take the edge off anxiety symptoms.

It isn't a cure, and it isn't a replacement for treatment. Here's what the studies show, how much walking it takes, and how to use a walk on a hard day. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to [walking and your mind](/learn/walking-mental-health/).)

## What does the research say about walking and anxiety?

The most direct evidence comes from a 2024 review that looked only at walking. It pooled 75 randomized trials with 8,636 people [1]. In the 26 trials that measured anxiety in adults, walking reduced anxiety symptoms compared with people who didn't walk [1].

On average, the effect was moderate in size (a standardized mean difference of -0.446) [1]. Results varied a lot from study to study, so some people may notice more change than others [1].

The same review also compared walking with other proven approaches, such as other types of exercise, yoga, or meditation. For anxiety, there was no significant difference between walking and those comparisons [1]. That doesn't mean walking replaces therapy. It means it's a real tool, not a placebo.

### What about exercise in general?

The bigger picture points the same way. A 2023 umbrella review in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* pulled together 97 reviews covering 1,039 trials and 128,119 people [2]. It found physical activity had medium effects on anxiety symptoms compared with usual care (a median effect size of -0.42) [2].

Those benefits showed up across many groups, including the general population, people with diagnosed mental health conditions, and people with chronic diseases [2]. Most of the included reviews were rated critically low on a standard quality checklist, so the findings come with some caveats [2].

Another review focused on people seen in general practice with diagnosed anxiety disorders or clearly raised anxiety. It included 15 trials and 675 people [3]. Aerobic exercise programs reduced anxiety compared with waiting-list groups, though the authors noted the small number of studies limited their conclusions [3].

## Does a single walk help, or do you need to walk regularly?

Both seem to matter, but in different ways.

**One walk.** A meta-analysis of 36 randomized trials looked at single exercise sessions and "state anxiety," which is how anxious you feel right now [4]. One bout of exercise led to a small but real improvement compared with not exercising [4]. The CDC also lists reduced short-term feelings of anxiety among the brain benefits that can happen right after a session of moderate-to-vigorous activity [5].

That effect may look small partly because many people in those studies weren't very anxious to begin with [4]. There's less room to improve when you already feel fine.

**Regular walking.** The 2024 walking review only included programs, not one-off walks [1]. So its results reflect what happens when walking becomes a habit over weeks. Being active regularly is also linked to a lower risk of developing anxiety in the first place, according to the CDC [5].

Think of it this way: a single walk can take the edge off today. A walking habit is what the bigger, longer-lasting benefits are built on.

## Are nature walks better for anxiety?

Possibly, though the evidence is mixed.

A 2022 review compared physical activity in natural settings, like parks and woods, with activity in urban settings [6]. Of the 16 studies that measured anxiety, 11 found a significant drop after activity in nature compared with activity in a city [6]. The pooled results also favored nature for anxiety [6]. But the studies had a high risk of bias, so the findings are less certain than they sound [6].

One well-known experiment gave healthy adults a 90-minute walk in either a natural setting or an urban one [7]. The nature walk reduced rumination, the habit of replaying the same negative thoughts, while the city walk didn't [7]. Rumination is linked to a higher risk of depression and other mental health problems, so this is a promising clue [7].

On the other hand, the 2024 walking review found that both indoor and outdoor walking reduced anxiety symptoms [1]. So if a treadmill or a mall loop is what you have, it still counts. Green space is a bonus, not a requirement.

## Why might walking help with anxiety?

Researchers don't know for sure, and no single explanation seems to cover it [8]. A few ideas have support:

- **A calmer stress response.** Regular aerobic exercise is linked to lower reactivity in the body's stress systems, including the one that controls cortisol [8].
- **Getting used to the feelings.** Anxiety often comes with a racing heart. Exercise produces similar sensations in a safe setting, which may help people become more tolerant of them [8].
- **A sense of control.** Finishing a walk is a small win. Building confidence in your ability to cope may help reduce worry [8].
- **A time out.** Stepping away from what's stressing you may play a part, though studies on this idea are mixed [8].

The honest answer is that it's probably a mix of these, and it may differ from person to person.

## How much walking helps with anxiety?

You don't need a perfect plan. In the 2024 walking review, both shorter walks (10 to 30 minutes) and longer ones (35 to 60 minutes) reduced anxiety symptoms [1]. Walking fewer than five times a week and five or more times a week both helped too [1]. The researchers didn't find that more walking was clearly better [1].

That's good news if you're starting from zero. Ten to 30 minutes, a few times a week, is a reasonable place to begin.

A few other patterns showed up:

- **Intensity may matter.** Higher-intensity programs tended to bring bigger improvements in the larger reviews [2][3]. Once easy walks feel comfortable, you can try picking up the pace.
- **Some structure helps.** Walking programs with guidance, like supervised walks or a warm-up and cool-down, did better for anxiety than walks with no instructions [1].
- **Shorter programs still work.** In the umbrella review, programs of 12 weeks or less reduced anxiety symptoms [2]. You don't need to commit to a year to notice a change.

## How to use a walk when you feel anxious

When anxiety spikes, a walk can be a simple thing to reach for. Try this:

1. **Start small.** Even 10 minutes counts. The goal is to move, not to finish a workout.
2. **Let your body set the pace at first.** If your heart is already pounding, start slow. You can speed up once you settle in.
3. **Notice what's around you.** Name a few things you can see or hear. It gives your attention somewhere to go besides your thoughts.
4. **Head for green if you can.** A park or a tree-lined street may give you a little extra lift [6][7].
5. **Leave the scroll behind.** Put your phone in your pocket, or play music or a podcast you find calming.
6. **Make it a routine, not just a rescue.** Regular walks are where most of the evidence is [1]. A daily walk at the same time is easier to keep than one you only take in a crisis.
7. **Track your streak.** A walking app or step counter makes small wins easy to see, which can help on days when motivation is low.

## Can walking replace therapy or medication?

No. Walking works best as one part of your care, not the whole plan.

The National Institute of Mental Health notes that treatment for generalized anxiety disorder typically involves psychotherapy, medication, or both [9]. It adds that stress management techniques, such as exercise, can reduce anxiety symptoms and enhance the effects of psychotherapy [9].

If you take medication for anxiety, keep taking it as prescribed. Don't stop or change it without talking to your doctor. Walking can sit alongside whatever treatment is working for you, and your care team can help you fit it in.

## When to get help

Talk to a doctor or mental health professional if anxiety is starting to get in the way of daily life, like work, school, sleep, or relationships. NIMH advises seeking professional help when anxiety starts to cause problems in everyday life [9]. You don't have to wait until things are at their worst.

If you or someone you know is in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text **988** to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the US. You can also chat online. It's free, confidential, and available 24/7 [10]. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.

## FAQ

### How long should I walk to calm anxiety?

Walks of 10 to 30 minutes reduced anxiety symptoms in trials, and longer walks did too [1]. Start with what feels doable.

### Is walking as good as other exercise for anxiety?

In one large review, walking reduced anxiety about as much as other active approaches, including other exercise [1]. The best exercise is the one you'll keep doing.

### Can walking make anxiety worse?

A faster heartbeat can feel like anxiety at first. Starting at an easy pace can help, and over time exercise may make those sensations feel less scary [8].

### Do I need to walk outside?

No. Both indoor and outdoor walking reduced anxiety symptoms in trials [1]. Nature may add a little extra, but the evidence is less certain [6].
