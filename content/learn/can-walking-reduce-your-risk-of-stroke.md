Stroke is common, and it can happen fast. In the U.S., someone has a stroke every 40 seconds, and more than 795,000 people have one each year [1]. It's also a leading cause of serious long-term disability [1].

The good news is that a lot of stroke risk comes from things you can change. Walking is one of the easiest of those things to start today. Here's what the research shows, how much walking seems to matter, and where the evidence has limits. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to [walking and your heart](/learn/walking-heart-health/).)

## Is stroke risk something you can actually change?

Much of it, yes. A huge study called INTERSTROKE compared about 13,400 people who'd just had a first stroke with a similar number of people who hadn't, across 32 countries [2].

Ten risk factors you can do something about were linked to about **90%** of stroke risk worldwide [2]. That held true in men and women, in every region, and at younger and older ages [2].

High blood pressure was the biggest one [2]. Regular physical activity was on the list too. People who were regularly active had about **40% lower odds** of stroke than people who weren't [2].

## What do studies on walking and stroke show?

Nobody has run a trial that assigns thousands of people to walk for 10 years and then counts strokes. So the best evidence comes from long-term studies that follow large groups of people. These show links, not proof, but the links are consistent.

### Walking more hours each week

A UK study followed more than 3,400 older men for 11 years [3]. They walked a median of 7 hours a week [3].

Compared with men who walked 0 to 3 hours a week, men who walked 8 to 14 hours had about a **one-third lower risk** of stroke [3]. Men who walked more than 22 hours a week had about **two-thirds lower risk**, though few men walked that much [3]. The more time men spent walking, the lower their risk tended to be, even after the researchers accounted for how fast they walked [3].

In a study of more than 39,000 U.S. women aged 45 and older, walking time and walking pace were both linked to a lower risk of stroke [4]. Vigorous exercise, on the other hand, wasn't clearly linked to stroke risk in that study [4]. So you don't need to go hard to be in the group that benefits.

### Walking faster

Pace seems to matter too. A 2020 review pooled 7 studies with more than 135,000 people, followed for a median of 8 years [5].

People in the fastest walking group had a **44% lower risk** of stroke than people in the slowest group [5]. The fastest group walked at a median of about 5.6 km/h (roughly 3.5 mph). The slowest walked at about 1.6 km/h (roughly 1 mph) [5].

Every 1 km/h faster (a bit over half a mile per hour) was linked to about a **13% lower risk** of stroke [5]. One catch: about 95% of the people in these studies were women, so the findings may apply less neatly to men [5].

## Do steps and sitting time matter?

Some newer studies skip the questionnaires and use activity trackers, which are more accurate.

In a U.S. study, more than 7,600 adults aged 45 and older wore a tracker for a week, then were followed for about 7 years [6]. Each extra hour a day of light activity, like easy walking, was linked to a **14% lower risk** of stroke [6]. Each extra hour of sitting was linked to a 14% higher risk [6]. Long, unbroken stretches of sitting carried extra risk [6].

The same study found something encouraging about short bursts. People who got the most moderate-to-vigorous activity in bouts of just 1 to 9 minutes had a lower stroke risk [6]. The authors suggest that swapping sitting for light activity, or even very short bouts of brisker movement, may lower stroke risk [6].

Step counts tell a similar story, though not for stroke alone. A 2023 analysis of 8 studies with more than 20,000 adults counted heart disease, stroke, and heart failure together [7]. In adults 60 and older, those who took the most steps had about half the risk of these events compared with those who took the fewest [7]. There was no clear link in adults under 60 [7]. For more on step targets, see [how many steps a day you need for heart health](/learn/how-many-steps-a-day-do-you-need-for-heart-health/).

## Why might walking protect your brain?

Most strokes, about 87%, happen when blood flow to the brain gets blocked [1]. Things that damage or narrow blood vessels raise that risk. High blood pressure is the biggest of them [2].

This is where walking has real trial evidence behind it. A Cochrane review of 73 trials found that regular walking lowered systolic blood pressure (the top number) by about **4 mmHg** [8]. The walking programs averaged about 153 minutes a week, mostly at moderate intensity, for about 15 weeks [8]. Read more in [Does walking lower blood pressure?](/learn/does-walking-lower-blood-pressure/)

The CDC also notes that physical activity can help you keep your cholesterol and blood pressure in a healthy range [9]. Walking may also help with [cholesterol](/learn/does-walking-lower-cholesterol/), though that effect is smaller.

## How much walking might help?

The studies don't point to one magic number, but they line up well with standard advice.

The CDC's stroke prevention advice for adults is 2 hours and 30 minutes a week of moderate activity, such as a brisk walk [9]. That's about 30 minutes, five days a week.

A big review of activity and disease found the largest drops in risk came at lower activity levels, with smaller gains after that [10]. People who were far more active than the minimum had about a **26% lower risk** of the most common type of stroke than people who were barely active [10]. In other words, getting started matters most, and more still helps.

### What counts as brisk?

The CDC lists walking at 2.5 miles per hour or faster as moderate activity [11]. An easier check is the talk test: you can talk, but you can't sing [11].

## How to start walking for stroke prevention

You don't need a plan from a coach. You need a habit. Try this:

1. **Start where you are.** If you're barely active, a 10-minute walk is a real start. Add a few minutes each week.
2. **Build toward 150 minutes a week.** Thirty minutes, five days a week, is a simple target [9]. Two or three shorter walks count too.
3. **Nudge your pace.** Aim for a speed where you can talk but not sing [11]. Faster walking is linked to lower stroke risk [5].
4. **Break up long sits.** Stand up and walk for a few minutes every hour or so. Long stretches of sitting were linked to higher risk [6].
5. **Use a step counter.** Tracking your steps or minutes makes it easier to notice your progress and keep going.
6. **Pair it with the other basics.** Not smoking, limiting alcohol, eating well, and keeping blood pressure in check all lower stroke risk too [9].

## When should you talk to your doctor?

Walking is safe for most people. But check with your doctor before you ramp up if any of these apply:

- You've had a stroke or a "mini-stroke" (TIA).
- You have high blood pressure, heart disease, atrial fibrillation, or diabetes.
- You take medicine for your heart, blood pressure, cholesterol, or blood sugar.
- You have balance problems or have fallen recently.
- You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.

If you take medicine, keep taking it as prescribed. Walking works alongside it, not instead of it. The CDC advises never stopping a medicine without talking to your doctor or pharmacist first [9].

### Know the warning signs

Walking lowers risk, but it doesn't remove it. Call 911 right away if you or someone near you has sudden numbness or weakness on one side, confusion, trouble speaking, trouble seeing, loss of balance, or a severe headache with no known cause [12]. Note the time symptoms started, and don't drive yourself to the hospital [12].

## FAQ

### Does walking help after a stroke?

It can be part of recovery, but stroke recovery plans are personal. Your care team will help decide what kind of activity is safe for you and when.

### Is fast walking better than slow walking?

Faster walking is linked to lower stroke risk, about 13% lower for every 1 km/h faster [5]. But walking more hours also counts, even at an average pace [3].

### Is a short walk worth it?

Yes. Even short bouts of brisk movement, under 10 minutes, were linked to lower stroke risk in one tracker study [6].

### Can young people have a stroke?

Yes. Risk rises with age, but strokes happen at any age. In 2014, 38% of people in the hospital for stroke were under 65 [1].
