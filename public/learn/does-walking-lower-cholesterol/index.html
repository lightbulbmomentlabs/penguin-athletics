Does walking lower cholesterol?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 15, 2026 5 min read

The short answer

A little, but the evidence is mixed. A review of 25 trials found regular walking lowered LDL ("bad") cholesterol by about 5%, while a later review of 32 walking trials found no clear change in blood fats. Walking helps your heart in other proven ways, but on its own it usually won't fix high cholesterol.

If your last blood test came back with a cholesterol number you didn't love, walking probably crossed your mind. It's free, it's easy, and it's good for your heart.

But cholesterol is the one place where walking's track record is honestly mixed. Some studies show a small drop in "bad" cholesterol. Others show almost nothing. Here's what the research says, why it disagrees, and how to get the most out of your walks. (For the full picture, see our guide to walking and your heart.)

What is cholesterol, and why does it matter?

Cholesterol is a waxy, fat-like substance your body needs in the right amounts [1]. It travels through your blood in tiny particles called lipoproteins [1].

The two you hear about most are LDL, often called "bad" cholesterol, and HDL, often called "good" cholesterol [1]. High LDL can build up as plaque in your arteries, which can lead to a heart attack or stroke [1]. HDL helps carry cholesterol to your liver so your body can get rid of it [1].

High cholesterol usually has no symptoms [2]. Most people only find out from a routine blood test [2].

How much does walking lower cholesterol?

The answer depends on which review you read.

A review of 25 randomized trials with 1,176 people found that walking lowered LDL cholesterol by about 5%, or about 5.5 mg/dL [3]. It also improved the ratio of total cholesterol to HDL by about 6% [3]. Total cholesterol, HDL, and triglycerides didn't change enough to be sure, though they moved in a helpful direction [3].

Then there's the newer evidence. A 2015 review of 32 walking trials in inactive adults found that walking improved fitness, blood pressure, waist size, and body weight, but "failed to alter blood lipids" [4].

Walking groups tell a similar story. A review of 42 studies of outdoor walking groups found a small, statistically significant drop in total cholesterol of 0.11 mmol/L [5]. The effect on HDL was less clear [5].

So which one is right?

Probably all of them, in a way. The honest summary is that walking may nudge your cholesterol in the right direction, but the change is small and doesn't show up in every study.

Compare that with blood pressure, where walking trials show a clearer drop [4]. (See does walking lower blood pressure?) Cholesterol is just a tougher number to move with walking alone.

Can walking raise your "good" HDL cholesterol?

It might, if you walk enough.

A review of 25 aerobic exercise trials found that regular exercise raised HDL by about 2.53 mg/dL on average [6]. That's modest. The trials weren't walking-only, but walking counts as aerobic exercise.

The same review found a minimum dose seemed to matter. The researchers estimated it took at least 120 minutes of exercise a week to raise HDL [6]. Each extra 10 minutes per session was linked to about a 1.4 mg/dL bigger rise in HDL [6]. How often or how hard people exercised didn't seem to matter as much as how long each session lasted [6].

That may explain some of the mixed walking results. Many walking programs are short or light, and the body may need a bigger weekly dose before blood fats change.

Does the amount of walking matter more than the speed?

For cholesterol, the amount seems to matter most.

In a well-known trial, 111 inactive adults with mild-to-moderate cholesterol problems were assigned to different exercise plans for about eight months [7]. One group walked the equivalent of about 12 miles a week at a moderate pace [7]. People were asked to keep their weight the same, so the results weren't just from weight loss [7].

Every exercise group did better than the group that didn't exercise, including the walkers [7]. The biggest improvements came from the highest amount of exercise [7]. The researchers concluded that the gains were tied to the amount of activity, not the intensity [7].

Is walking as good as running for cholesterol?

When people burn the same amount of energy, it may be.

A large study followed about 33,000 runners and 16,000 walkers for about six years [8]. Walkers and runners who used similar amounts of energy had similar drops in their risk of being diagnosed with high cholesterol [8]. For each extra unit of daily activity, walking was linked to about a 7% lower risk of high cholesterol, and running to about 4% [8].

This was an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof that walking caused the difference. But it suggests walking farther can stand in for running faster. Walking just takes more time to burn the same energy.

How to walk for healthier cholesterol

Walking won't do the job alone, but it's a strong part of the plan. Here's how to make it count:

  1. Aim for at least 150 minutes a week. U.S. guidelines say adults need 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity, like brisk walking, each week [9]. That's about 30 minutes, five days a week.
  2. Go longer, not just faster. Session length seemed to matter more than intensity for HDL [6]. If you can, stretch a 20-minute walk to 30 or 40.
  3. Build up over time. The biggest cholesterol gains came with the most total activity [7]. Add a few minutes each week.
  4. Count every bit. Any amount of physical activity has some health benefits, and short walks add up [9].
  5. Pair it with food changes. The NHLBI recommends eating plans that limit saturated fat and add whole grains, fruits, vegetables, and nuts to lower LDL [10].
  6. Add strength training. U.S. guidelines also recommend muscle-strengthening activity at least 2 days a week [9]. The NHLBI notes resistance training may lower cholesterol in postmenopausal women [10].
  7. Track your walks. A step counter or walking app shows your weekly minutes adding up, which makes it easier to stick with.

When should you talk to your doctor?

Talk to your doctor before starting a new exercise plan if you have high cholesterol, heart disease, or diabetes, or if you've had a heart attack or stroke. The NHLBI advises asking your provider what level of activity is right for you [10].

If you take cholesterol medicine, like a statin, keep taking it as prescribed. Walking works alongside medicine, not instead of it. The NHLBI recommends keeping up healthy lifestyle changes while taking cholesterol medicine [10].

Stop and get help right away if you have chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath while walking.

The only way to know whether your habits are working is a blood test. Ask your doctor how often you should get your cholesterol checked.

FAQ

How long until walking changes cholesterol?

There's no set timeline. One well-known trial ran about eight months [7], so give it a few months and recheck with your doctor.

Is walking 30 minutes a day enough?

It meets the 150 minutes a week U.S. guidelines recommend [9], and it's above the 120-minute weekly mark linked to higher HDL [6]. For bigger changes, you may need more activity plus diet changes.

Does losing weight help cholesterol?

Yes. The NHLBI says adults with higher weight can lower LDL and raise HDL by losing just 3% to 5% of their weight [10].

Can I stop my statin if I walk more?

No, not on your own. Keep taking your medicine as prescribed and talk to your doctor before changing anything [10].

  • Walking vs running: which is better for your heart?

    Heart · 6 min readBoth are good for your heart, and neither clearly wins. In a study of about 49,000 walkers and runners, burning the same amount of energy by walking or running led to similar drops in the risk of high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, and possibly heart disease. Running gets you there in less time, while walking takes longer but is easier to start and stick with.

  • How many steps a day do you need for heart health?

    Heart · 6 min readYou probably don't need 10,000. In large studies, people who took more steps had a lower risk of heart disease, with most of the benefit showing up by about 7,000 to 9,000 steps a day. In adults 60 and older, 6,000 to 9,000 steps a day was linked to a 40% to 50% lower risk of heart disease than about 2,000 steps.

  • Does walking lower blood pressure?

    Heart · 6 min readYes. A large Cochrane review of 73 trials found that regular walking lowered systolic blood pressure (the top number) by about 4 mmHg on average. Most of the walking programs in those trials averaged around 150 minutes of moderate walking a week for a few months.

  • How to walk with good posture

    Basics · 6 min readStand tall, keep your chin level and your eyes about 10 to 20 feet ahead, relax your shoulders down and back, let your arms swing naturally, and roll smoothly from heel to toe without overstriding. Letting your arms swing matters: holding them still took about 12% more energy in one lab study. There's no single perfect posture, so aim for comfortable and relaxed, not rigid.
