Is walking good for depression?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 10, 2026 7 min read

The short answer

Yes, walking can help. A 2024 review of 44 trials in adults found walking reduced depression symptoms compared with not walking, and the effect was bigger in people who were already depressed. Walking works best as part of your care, alongside therapy or medication when you need them, not as a replacement.

When you're depressed, "just go for a walk" can feel like the most annoying advice in the world. Getting off the couch takes energy you may not have. So it's fair to ask whether it's even worth it.

The research says it is. Walking has been tested in dozens of trials for depression, and it costs nothing. It isn't a cure, and it isn't a substitute for treatment. But it's a real tool, and you can start with a lot less than you think. (For the bigger picture on mood, anxiety, and sleep, see our guide to walking and your mind.)

What do the studies show about walking and depression?

The most direct evidence comes from a 2024 review that looked only at walking. It pooled 75 randomized trials with 8,636 people [1]. In the 44 trials that measured depression symptoms in adults, walking reduced those symptoms compared with people who didn't walk [1].

The effect was moderate in size on average (a standardized mean difference of -0.591) [1]. It also held up across many kinds of walking, including different weekly frequencies and walk lengths, indoors and outdoors, and alone or in groups [1].

Does it help people who are actually depressed?

Yes, and possibly even more. In that same review, people who were depressed at the start of the trials improved more than people who weren't [1]. That estimate came from just 5 trials, though, so it's less certain [1].

A 2024 review in The BMJ focused only on people with major depression. It included 218 trials with 14,170 participants [2]. Walking or jogging led to moderate reductions in depression compared with control conditions like usual care [2]. Exercise also seemed to work about equally well for people with different starting levels of depression [2].

The authors were upfront about the weak spots. Only one study met strict criteria for low risk of bias, so confidence in the walking or jogging result was rated low [2].

What about exercise in general?

Cochrane, a group known for strict reviews, updated its review of exercise for depression in 2026. It now includes 73 trials with at least 4,985 adults [3]. In 57 trials, exercise may reduce depression symptoms moderately compared with no treatment or a control [3].

When the reviewers kept only the most carefully run trials, the benefit was smaller, but it was still there [3]. They rated the overall evidence as low certainty and noted that long-term follow-up was rare [3]. In plain English: exercise helps, but researchers still need better and longer studies.

A separate 2023 review of 41 trials found large effects of exercise on depression symptoms, and moderate effects when it looked only at low-risk-of-bias studies [4]. Supervised exercise showed large effects too [4].

Is walking as good as therapy or antidepressants?

In head-to-head trials, exercise looks about as good. But those trials are few and small, so be careful with this one.

The 2026 Cochrane review found ten trials that compared exercise with psychological therapy. There was probably little to no difference between them on depression symptoms [3]. Five trials compared exercise with medication, and there may be little to no difference there either [3]. The authors stress that this conclusion rests on a few small trials [3].

The walking review found the same pattern. Walking reduced depression symptoms about as much as other proven approaches, such as other exercise, yoga, or therapy [1].

That doesn't mean you should swap your treatment for a walk. Exercise added to medication also brought clinically meaningful benefits in The BMJ review [2]. The more useful takeaway is that walking can stand alongside your treatment as a real part of your plan.

How much walking helps with depression?

Less than you might expect. In the walking review, both shorter walks (10 to 30 minutes) and longer ones (35 to 60 minutes) reduced depression symptoms [1]. Walking fewer than five times a week and five or more times a week both helped [1].

Long-term studies point the same way. A 2022 review in JAMA Psychiatry followed more than 191,000 adults in 15 studies [5]. People whose activity added up to about 2.5 hours of brisk walking a week had a 25% lower risk of depression than people who reported no activity [5].

Even half that, about 1.25 hours a week, was linked to an 18% lower risk [5]. The biggest differences came from going from no activity to some activity [5]. These are observational studies, so they show a link, not proof. People who feel well may also find it easier to move.

What about steps?

A 2024 review of 33 studies and 96,173 adults looked at daily step counts [6]. In the long-term studies, people who took 7,000 or more steps a day had a 31% lower risk of depression than people who took fewer [6]. Each extra 1,000 steps a day was linked to a 9% lower risk [6]. Most of the included studies were snapshots in time, and the authors called for more long-term research [6].

Does pace matter?

Some. In The BMJ review, the benefits grew with intensity [2]. Light activity like walking still brought clinically meaningful improvements, but vigorous exercise like running had stronger expected effects [2].

So any walk counts. If an easy stroll is what you can manage today, take it. On better days, try picking up the pace until you can talk but not sing.

Does it matter where or with whom you walk?

Both indoor and outdoor walking reduced depression symptoms in trials, and so did walking in groups and walking alone [1]. A treadmill on a rainy day still counts.

Walking with others. A review of outdoor walking groups found 42 studies with 1,843 people [7]. Walking groups reduced depression scores, and none of the studies reported notable harmful side effects [7]. A group also gives you two things depression often takes away: connection and someone expecting you to show up.

Walking in green space. In one experiment, healthy adults who took a 90-minute walk in nature had less rumination afterward, while people who walked in a city didn't [8]. Rumination, the loop of replaying the same negative thoughts, is linked to a higher risk of depression [8]. It was a small study in people without depression, so treat it as a promising clue, not proof.

How to start walking when you're depressed

Depression can make the first step the hardest one. These ideas are built for low-energy days:

  1. Shrink the goal. Ten minutes counts. In trials, walks of 10 to 30 minutes helped [1]. On a rough day, "walk to the corner and back" is a win.
  2. Don't wait to feel like it. NIMH notes that doing things you used to enjoy can improve your mood even if you don't feel like doing them [9].
  3. Make it a plan, not a decision. Pick the same time each day, like right after coffee or lunch. Fewer choices mean fewer chances to talk yourself out of it.
  4. Bring someone. Walks with a friend, a neighbor, or a local walking group give you company and a reason to show up [7].
  5. Head for trees if you can. A park or leafy street may give a little extra lift [8].
  6. Count the walk, not the mood. Some days a walk helps a lot, and some days it doesn't seem to. Track that you went. The trials tested regular walking over weeks to months, not one-off walks [1].

Should walking replace your treatment?

No. Walking works best as one part of your care.

NIMH says depression treatment typically involves psychotherapy, medication, or both [9]. It also lists physical activity among the things that may help, noting that just 30 minutes a day of walking can boost your mood [9].

If you take medication for depression, keep taking it as prescribed. Don't stop or change it without talking to your doctor. If you'd like exercise to play a bigger role, bring it up with your care team. They can help you fit it into a plan that works for you.

If anxiety comes along with your low mood, our article on whether walking helps anxiety covers that side of things.

When to get help

Talk to a doctor or mental health professional if you've felt down or lost interest in things most of the day, nearly every day, for two weeks or more. NIMH uses that two-week mark as part of how depression is diagnosed [9]. You don't have to wait until things get worse. Depression is treatable.

If you or someone you know is in crisis or thinking about suicide, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the US, or chat online. It's free, confidential, and available 24/7 [10]. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.

FAQ

How long until walking helps my mood?

Walking trials ran from 10 days to 18 months, most for 3 to 6 months [1]. Programs under 3 months helped too, so give it a few weeks before you judge [1].

Is walking enough on its own?

It can help on its own, but treatment usually means therapy, medication, or both [9]. Walking fits best as one part of that plan. Ask your doctor what's right for you.

Can walking prevent depression?

People who are more active tend to have a lower risk of depression [5][6]. Those studies show a link, not proof that walking prevents it.

Is running better than walking for depression?

More intense exercise had somewhat stronger effects in one large review, but walking still helped in a meaningful way [2]. The best option is the one you'll keep doing.
