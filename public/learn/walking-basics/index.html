Walking basics: the complete beginner's guide

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 6, 2026 12 min read

The short answer

Adults need at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and brisk walking counts. That's about 30 minutes a day, five days a week, at a pace where you can talk but not sing. If you're starting from zero, any amount helps, so begin small and build up gradually.

Walking is the easiest way most people can get more active. You already know how to do it. You don't need a gym, a coach, or special gear. And the health guidelines count it as real exercise.

This guide covers the basics in one place: how much to walk, how fast, how many steps to aim for, how to start from zero, and how to stay safe. It also covers the part most people struggle with, which is turning walking into a habit that lasts.

If you're starting from the couch, that's fine. Most of the advice here is written with you in mind.

How much walking do adults need?

The U.S. guidelines say adults need 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week [1]. Brisk walking is one of the most common ways to get there. If you prefer harder exercise, 75 minutes of vigorous activity a week also meets the goal, or you can mix the two [1].

The full Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans give a range: 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate activity [2]. The 150 minutes is the starting target. Doing more brings additional benefits [2].

A common way to hit 150 minutes is 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week [1]. But you don't have to do it that way. You can spread activity across the week and break it into smaller chunks [1].

Do short walks count?

Yes. The earlier version of the guidelines only counted activity done in bouts of at least 10 minutes. The second edition removed that rule [3]. It says any amount of physical activity has some health benefits [3].

The CDC puts it simply: some physical activity is better than none [1]. A 5-minute walk around the block is not a waste of time. It's a start.

What else do adults need?

The guidelines also call for muscle-strengthening activity on at least 2 days a week [1]. Walking doesn't cover that part, so it's worth adding things like body-weight exercises or lifting weights over time.

Older adults should also do activities that include balance training, along with aerobic and muscle-strengthening activity [2]. The guidelines also tell adults to move more and sit less throughout the day [3].

Who gains the most?

People who are the least active have the most to gain. The guidelines say that people doing the least physical activity benefit most from even modest increases [2]. If you're starting from zero, your first few weeks of walking may matter more than anyone's.

What does "brisk walking" actually mean?

"Brisk" is the word you'll see everywhere, but it can feel vague. Here are three simple ways to tell.

The talk test

This is the easiest check. At moderate intensity, you can talk, but you can't sing [4]. If you can only say a few words before pausing for a breath, you've moved into vigorous intensity [4].

So if you can belt out a whole song, pick up the pace. If you can't finish a sentence, ease off a little. You want the zone in between.

The pace

The CDC lists walking briskly at 2.5 miles per hour or faster as moderate-intensity activity [4]. The NHS describes a brisk walk as about 3 miles an hour, which is faster than a stroll [5].

You don't need to measure your speed every time. Most people learn what "brisk" feels like after a few walks. The talk test works anywhere, even without a phone.

How it feels

Brisk walking should feel like purposeful effort. You're breathing a bit harder and your heart is beating faster, but you're not gasping. It's the pace of someone who's running a little late, not someone running for a bus.

If brisk walking feels like too much at first, that's normal. Walk at whatever pace feels comfortable, and the brisk part will come as you get fitter. Some walking is better than none [1].

How many steps a day should you aim for?

You've probably heard of 10,000 steps a day. That number is widely promoted, but researchers have noted there was little evidence behind it [6].

The best evidence on steps so far comes from a 2022 meta-analysis that pooled data from 15 studies and 47,471 adults [6]. It looked at how daily steps were linked to the risk of dying from any cause over about 7 years [6].

Here's what it found:

  • People who took more steps each day had a lower risk of death, and the benefit kept growing up to a point [6].
  • For adults 60 and older, the risk kept dropping until about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day [6].
  • For adults younger than 60, it kept dropping until about 8,000 to 10,000 steps a day [6].
  • Compared with the least active group (a median of about 3,500 steps a day), people averaging about 5,800 steps had a 40% lower risk of death [6].

This was an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof that steps alone caused the difference. But the pattern is clear: more steps is linked to better outcomes, and you don't need to hit 10,000 to see benefits. For the full story, read how many steps a day you need to live longer.

Picking your own step goal

The best step goal is one that's a little above what you do now. Check your phone or tracker for your average over the last week or two. Then aim for a modest bump, like 500 to 1,000 more steps a day, and raise it again once that feels normal.

Having a goal seems to help. In a review of pedometer studies, having a step goal (such as 10,000 steps a day) was an important predictor of people becoming more active [7]. In the randomized trials, people using pedometers added about 2,491 more steps a day than people who didn't [7].

How do you start walking from zero?

If you haven't been active for a while, the most important rule is to start small and build up gradually. The NHS advises people who aren't very active, but can walk, to increase their walking distance gradually [5].

A sample beginner program from the National Institutes of Health started with just 15 minutes of total walking in the first two weeks: 5 minutes slow, 5 minutes brisk, and 5 minutes slow [8]. It added about 5 minutes of brisk walking every two weeks [8]. By weeks 19 and 20, the total reached 60 minutes [8].

The same guide suggests that if you walk fewer than three times a week, you should wait more than two weeks before adding more time [8]. As walking gets easier, you can go faster and farther, or add hills and stairs [8].

A simple plan to try

Here's a way to begin, based on the advice above:

  1. Week 1–2: Walk 10 to 15 minutes, most days. Any pace that feels comfortable is fine.
  2. Find your baseline. Look at your step count for these first weeks. That's your starting point, not a grade.
  3. Week 3–4: Add 5 minutes to each walk, or add one more walk a week. Try a few minutes at a brisk pace in the middle [8].
  4. Keep adding slowly. Every two weeks or so, add a little more time [8]. Work toward 150 minutes a week [1].
  5. Split it up if you need to. Two 15-minute walks count just as much as one 30-minute walk [1], [3].
  6. Repeat what works. Walk at the same time and place each day so it starts to feel automatic [9].

How should you walk? Form, posture, and shoes

You don't need to overthink your form. But a few cues can make walking more comfortable, especially on longer walks.

Good walking posture

Guidance from the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases suggests [8]:

  • Keep your chin up and your shoulders slightly back and relaxed.
  • Look forward, not at the ground.
  • Keep your back straight, not arched forward or backward.
  • Let your heel touch the ground first, then roll your weight forward.
  • Swing your arms naturally.

If you're walking while looking at your phone, you're probably breaking at least two of these. Save the scrolling for later.

Choosing shoes

You don't need expensive shoes to start. The NHS says any shoes or trainers that are comfortable, supportive, and don't cause blisters will do [5].

If you're shopping for walking shoes, the NIDDK suggests looking for good arch support, a firm and well-cushioned heel, and nonskid, flexible soles [8]. If you start walking more often or on rougher trails, you might want proper walking shoes and a waterproof jacket [5].

What to bring

For short walks near home, you just need your shoes and maybe your phone. For longer walks, the NHS suggests bringing water, a snack, an extra layer, sunscreen, and a sun hat [5].

Do you need to warm up before walking?

Walking is gentle, so you don't need a long routine. The simplest warm-up is to start slowly.

The NIH beginner program opens each session with 5 minutes of slow walking before speeding up, and ends with 5 minutes of slower walking to cool down [8]. That's it.

Stretching is optional. If you want to stretch, the NIDDK suggests doing it after you've warmed up or cooled down [8]. Move slowly, go only as far as feels comfortable, and avoid bouncing or holding your breath [8].

How do you stay safe while walking?

For most people, walking is a low-risk way to get active. An expert panel from the American College of Sports Medicine noted that there's considerable evidence that exercise is safe for most people [10]. Still, a few precautions make a big difference.

Hot weather

Heat can make you sick if your body gets too hot. The CDC lists signs of overheating that include muscle cramps, unusually heavy sweating, shortness of breath, dizziness, headaches, weakness, and nausea [11].

On hot days, the CDC suggests doing outdoor activities during the coolest parts of the day, like the early morning or evening [11]. Carry water and drink it throughout the day, and rest in the shade when you can [11]. If you notice those warning signs, stop, get somewhere cool, and know when to get medical care [11].

Walking in the dark

If you walk early or late, make yourself easy to see. The NIDDK recommends wearing a reflective vest or brightly colored clothing when it's dark outside [8]. Stick to well-lit routes you know, and keep one earbud out so you can hear traffic.

Pain and symptoms that mean stop

Some symptoms are a reason to stop right away. The CDC lists the major symptoms of a heart attack as [12]:

  • Chest pain or discomfort
  • Feeling weak, light-headed, or faint
  • Pain or discomfort in the jaw, neck, or back
  • Pain or discomfort in one or both arms or shoulders
  • Shortness of breath

If you notice these in yourself or someone else, call 911 immediately [12]. Don't try to walk it off.

Heart problems during exercise are often preceded by warning signs or symptoms [10], so take them seriously even if they go away. Tell your doctor about them before your next walk.

For aches in your feet, knees, or hips, be cautious. If a pain is sharp, gets worse as you keep going, or makes you limp, stop for the day. If it doesn't settle, get it checked by a doctor or physical therapist.

When should you talk to a doctor first?

Most healthy people can start walking without seeing a doctor first. The ACSM updated its screening advice partly because the old rules could send too many people to the doctor, which risked becoming a barrier to getting active [10].

The ACSM now bases screening on three things: how active you are now, whether you have signs or symptoms or known heart, metabolic, or kidney disease, and how hard you plan to exercise [10]. The risks linked to exercise also go down as people become more active and fit [10].

Talk to your doctor before starting a new walking routine if any of these apply:

  • You have heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, or kidney disease [10].
  • You've had chest pain, dizziness, or fainting, especially during activity [10].
  • You're pregnant or recently gave birth.
  • You're recovering from an injury or surgery.
  • You've had falls or have trouble with balance.

If you take medicine for any condition, keep taking it as prescribed. Walking works alongside treatment, not instead of it. For heart-specific advice, see our guide to walking and your heart.

How do you build a walking habit that sticks?

Most people don't quit walking because it's hard. They quit because life gets busy and walking never became automatic. The good news is that habits can be learned.

A well-known study from University College London asked people to repeat a new healthy behavior once a day in the same setting. On average, it took 66 days for the behavior to reach its peak level of automaticity, though there was considerable variation from person to person [9]. So if walking still feels like effort after two weeks, that's normal.

The same study had another encouraging finding. Missing the occasional day did not seriously harm the habit-forming process [9]. One skipped walk is not the end of your streak in any way that matters.

What helps a habit form

The researchers' advice boils down to a few ideas [9]:

  • Repeat the same thing in the same context. Walking at the same time and place helps it become automatic.
  • Tie it to a cue. Pick something that already happens every day, like finishing lunch or getting home from work. A cue within an existing routine is a stable place to start.
  • Pick a cue you'll notice. It should be something you run into often and consistently.

In practice, that might look like: "After I finish my morning coffee, I walk around the block." Small and specific beats big and vague.

Making it easier to keep going

A few other things can help:

  • Lower the bar on bad days. A 5-minute walk keeps the habit alive [3].
  • Bring someone along. A friend, partner, or dog makes walks feel less like a chore.
  • Notice how you feel. Physical activity has immediate benefits, like lower anxiety and better sleep [3]. Read more about walking and mental health.

How accurate are step counters?

Step counts from your phone or watch are a useful guide, but they're not perfect. The good news is that for normal walking, most popular devices do a decent job.

A small study compared iPhones and a wrist tracker to video recordings of people walking. The iPhone's step count was off by less than 3% for normal and free walking [13]. It didn't matter much whether the phone was in a pants pocket, a shoulder bag, or a backpack [13].

A larger review of 67 studies looked at Fitbit devices. Fitbits met an acceptable accuracy standard for step counts about half the time [14]. They tended to undercount steps in lab tests and overcount them in everyday life [14]. The reviewers concluded that step counts in adults without mobility limitations were the main measure worth relying on. Fitbits were unlikely to measure calories burned accurately in any test [14].

What this means for you

  • Use your step count as a trend, not an exact number. If your average goes up over a month, that's real progress.
  • Stick to one device. Comparing a phone to a watch will give different numbers. Pick one and track changes over time.
  • Don't trust calorie counts too much. Step counts are much more reliable than calorie estimates [14].

Tracking can be motivating, too. In the pedometer review mentioned earlier, people who used pedometers increased their activity by 26.9% over where they started [7].

Do you need rest days from walking?

The guidelines are written as a weekly total, and you can spread activity across the week however works for you [1]. Some people walk every day. Others walk five days a week, which is the CDC's own example [1].

That said, rest still has a place, especially when you're new:

  • If you're just starting, a few easy days a week give your body time to adjust. The NIH beginner program builds up over months, not days [8].
  • Easy days count. A slow, short walk on a "rest" day still adds to your movement for the week, and the guidelines encourage moving more and sitting less [3].
  • Listen to aches. If something hurts in a way that doesn't fade, take a day or two off and get it checked if it doesn't improve.

FAQ

Is walking enough exercise on its own?

Brisk walking can meet the 150-minute aerobic goal [1]. Adults also need muscle-strengthening activity on at least 2 days a week, which walking doesn't cover [1].

Is 10,000 steps a day necessary?

No. In a large meta-analysis, the lower risk of death leveled off around 6,000 to 8,000 steps for people 60 and older and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults [6].

How long until walking feels like a habit?

In one study, it took an average of 66 days for a daily behavior to become automatic, with a lot of variation between people [9].

Can I walk every day?

For most people, yes. The guidelines count total minutes across the week, so daily walks are an easy way to get there [1]. Build up gradually [8], and talk to your doctor first if you have a health condition [10].
