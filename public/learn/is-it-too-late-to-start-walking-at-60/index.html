Is it too late to start walking at 60?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 22, 2026 6 min read

The short answer

No, it isn't too late. Large studies link becoming more active in midlife and later to a lower risk of early death, and a review of adults 60 and older linked even small amounts of moderate activity to a 22% lower risk. In a trial of adults aged 70 to 89, a walking-based program lowered the risk of losing the ability to walk about a quarter mile.

A lot of people quietly assume the window has closed. If you weren't active at 30 or 40, the thinking goes, the damage is done.

The research says otherwise. When scientists follow people who start moving later in life, those people tend to do better than people who stay inactive. And trials in people well past 60 show that walking can protect the things that matter most as you age, like getting around on your own.

Here's what the evidence shows, what it can't tell you, and how to start safely. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to walking and longevity.)

Do you still get benefits if you start late?

Yes, according to several large studies that tracked how people's activity changed over time.

A U.S. study of more than 315,000 adults asked people how active they had been at different ages. Some had been inactive for most of their adult life, then picked up activity between ages 40 and 61. Compared with people who stayed inactive, they had a 35% lower risk of dying during the study, about the same as people who had been active all along. The researchers concluded that midlife is not too late to start.

A UK study of about 14,600 adults aged 40 to 79 found a similar pattern. Going from inactive to meeting the minimum activity guidelines over about five years was linked to a 24% lower risk of death from any cause. The link held even for people who already had heart disease or cancer.

What about people already past 65?

The evidence there is older but encouraging. A study of more than 9,500 women aged 65 and up compared those who stayed sedentary with those who became more active over about six years. The women who increased their activity had about half the death rate of women who stayed sedentary.

That study also found the benefit was weaker in women 75 and older and in those in poor health. So the gains may shrink with age and illness, but the study still found a lower risk for women who increased their activity.

What these studies can and can't tell you

These are observational studies. They show that people who became more active tended to live longer, not that activity alone caused it. People who start exercising may differ in other ways too. The researchers adjusted for things like smoking, weight, and health conditions, but they can't rule out every difference.

How much walking does it take after 60?

Less than you might think. A review of nine studies with more than 122,000 adults aged 60 and older looked at how much moderate activity people did each week. Even a low amount, below what guidelines recommend, was linked to a 22% lower risk of death compared with being inactive. People who met the guidelines had a 28% lower risk, and the benefit kept growing with more activity.

Step counts tell a similar story. A large analysis of 15 step-counter studies found that for adults 60 and older, the risk of dying early kept falling as steps rose, then leveled off at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day. For adults under 60, it leveled off higher, at 8,000 to 10,000. (More on this in how many steps a day to live longer.)

The CDC recommends that adults 65 and older get at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, such as brisk walking. That could be 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week. If that's out of reach, the CDC says to be as active as your abilities and conditions allow, and that some activity is better than none.

Can walking keep you independent?

This is where some of the strongest evidence lives, because it comes from a randomized trial.

The LIFE study enrolled 1,635 inactive adults aged 70 to 89 who had some physical limitations but could still walk about a quarter mile (400 meters). Their average age was about 79. Half joined a program built around walking, with a goal of 150 minutes a week, plus strength, flexibility, and balance exercises. The other half went to health education workshops.

Over about 2.6 years, 30.1% of the activity group lost the ability to walk a quarter mile, compared with 35.5% of the education group. That's an 18% lower risk. Serious health events weren't significantly different between the two groups.

For a lot of people, walking to the mailbox, the store, or a grandkid's game is the whole point. This trial suggests that starting in your 70s or 80s can help protect that.

Is it good for your brain, too?

There's promising evidence. In a one-year randomized trial, 120 adults aged 55 to 80 (average age in the mid-60s) were assigned to either a walking group three days a week or a stretching and toning group.

The walking group's hippocampus, a part of the brain tied to memory, grew by about 2%. That roughly reversed one to two years of normal age-related shrinkage, and their spatial memory improved. In the stretching group, the hippocampus kept shrinking.

It's one trial, so it's not proof that walking prevents memory loss. But it shows the aging brain can still respond to exercise.

Does walking prevent falls?

On its own, maybe not. This is worth knowing before you start.

A Cochrane review of 59 trials in adults 60 and older found that exercise programs cut the rate of falls by 23%. Balance and functional exercises cut falls by 24%, and programs that combined balance with strength training probably did even better. But the reviewers were uncertain whether programs built mainly on walking prevent falls.

So walking is a great base. For falls, add balance and strength work too. The CDC recommends muscle-strengthening activities at least 2 days a week, plus activities that improve balance, for adults 65 and older.

How to start walking at 60 (or 70, or 80)

Start where you are, not where you think you should be. Try this:

  1. Check in with your doctor first if you have any of the conditions listed in the next section.
  2. Start small. Five to ten minutes at an easy pace is a real start. Do it most days.
  3. Add a few minutes each week. Build toward 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week, the CDC's example for older adults.
  4. Pick up the pace when it feels easy. Brisk walking counts as moderate activity. You should be able to talk, but not sing.
  5. Use your steps as a guide. Find your daily average, then add about 1,000. Many older adults may get most of the benefit by around 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day.
  6. Add balance and strength. The CDC suggests balance activities like walking heel-to-toe or standing up from a chair. Aim for strength work at least 2 days a week.
  7. Make it social or scenic. A walking buddy, a dog, or a nice route makes it easier to keep going. (If exercise has never been your thing, see how to start walking when you hate exercise.)

When should you talk to your doctor first?

Walking is safe for most people, but check with your doctor before you start if:

  • You have heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, or another long-term condition.
  • You've fallen recently, feel unsteady, or use a cane or walker.
  • You have joint pain, arthritis, or an injury that walking makes worse.
  • You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.

Your doctor can help you choose a safe starting point and pace. If you have a health condition, the CDC's advice is to be as active as your abilities and conditions allow.

FAQ

Is 70 too late to start walking?

No. In the LIFE trial, adults aged 70 to 89 who joined a walking-based program had a lower risk of losing the ability to walk a quarter mile.

How long until I notice a difference?

It varies. In one trial, brain changes showed up over a year of walking three days a week. Many of the benefits in this article come from months to years of steady walking.

Should I walk fast or slow?

Start at whatever pace is comfortable. Over time, aim for some brisk walking, which counts as moderate activity.

Is walking enough exercise after 60?

Walking covers the aerobic part. Guidelines for adults 65 and older also call for strength and balance work, which matters for preventing falls.

    Longevity · 6 min readPeople who naturally walk faster tend to live longer. In a pooled study of 34,485 adults 65 and older, each 0.1 meter per second of extra walking speed was linked to a 12% lower risk of death, and speeds of about 1.0 meter per second (around 2.2 mph) or faster went with longer-than-expected survival. Speed is partly a sign of overall health, so it shows a strong link rather than proof that walking faster adds years.

  • Is the 10,000 steps rule real?

    Longevity · 6 min readThe 10,000 steps rule is not based on science. It traces back to a marketing campaign in Japan. Large studies find health benefits start at as few as about 2,500 steps a day, with the drop in risk of early death leveling off at around 7,000 to 9,000 steps for most adults, though 10,000 is still a fine goal if you can reach it.

  • How many steps a day to live longer?

    Longevity · 7 min readIn a pooled analysis of 15 studies, the risk of dying early kept falling as steps rose, then leveled off at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for adults 60 and older and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults. You don't need 10,000 to benefit: a 2025 review linked 7,000 steps a day to a 47% lower risk of death than 2,000 steps.

    Mind · 6 min readProbably, yes, though no one has studied the hot girl walk by name. Walks as short as 10 to 15 minutes reliably lift mood in experiments, and a 2024 review of 75 trials found regular walking reduced depression and anxiety symptoms in adults. The gratitude and playlist parts may add a little, but the walking does most of the work.
