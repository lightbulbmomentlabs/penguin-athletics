What is a good daily step goal?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 9, 2026 6 min read

The short answer

There's no single magic number. Large studies link about 7,000 steps a day to much lower health risks than 2,000, with benefits leveling off around 6,000 to 8,000 steps for adults 60 and older and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults. The best personal goal starts from your current daily average and climbs a little at a time.

Most step counters come with a goal already set, usually 10,000. That number is easy to remember. But it wasn't picked by a study, and it may not be the right goal for you.

The good news is that researchers have now tracked step counts in hundreds of thousands of people. That data gives a much clearer answer to what a "good" step goal looks like, and it's friendlier than you might think.

Here's what the research says, plus a simple way to set a goal you'll actually keep. (For the full beginner's overview, see our guide to walking basics.)

Is there one right number of steps?

No. Health benefits rise gradually as steps go up, so there isn't a single line where walking suddenly "counts."

A 2025 review in The Lancet Public Health pooled 57 studies from 35 groups of people [1]. Compared with 2,000 steps a day, 7,000 steps a day was linked to a 47% lower risk of death from any cause [1]. It was also linked to lower risks of heart disease, dementia, type 2 diabetes, depressive symptoms, and falls [1].

The authors concluded that 10,000 steps can still be a good target for people who are already active. But 7,000 steps a day may be a more realistic goal for some people, and it's still linked to meaningful health gains [1].

Does the right goal depend on your age?

It seems to. A 2022 analysis of 15 studies and more than 47,000 adults found that the risk of dying early kept falling as steps rose, then leveled off [2]:

  • at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for adults 60 and older
  • at about 8,000 to 10,000 steps a day for adults younger than 60

These are observational studies. They show that people who walk more tend to be healthier, not that steps alone are the reason. The authors note that some of the link could still come from other differences between people [1].

How few steps still make a difference?

Fewer than you'd guess. A 2023 meta-analysis of 12 studies and about 111,000 people looked for the smallest step count linked to a benefit [3].

Compared with 2,000 steps a day, the risk of death was already lower at about 2,500 steps a day, and the risk of heart disease was lower at about 2,700 [3]. Risk kept dropping with more steps, up to about 8,800 steps a day for death from any cause and about 7,100 for heart disease [3].

So if you're at 2,000 steps today, getting to 3,000 isn't a consolation prize. It's where some of the biggest changes show up. (For more on this, see how many steps a day you need to live longer.)

What counts as low, average, or active?

Researchers have used a rough scale to describe step counts in healthy adults [4]:

  • Under 5,000 a day: a sedentary lifestyle
  • 5,000 to 7,499: "low active," typical of daily life without exercise
  • 7,500 to 9,999: "somewhat active," likely including some walking or exercise on purpose
  • 10,000 or more: "active"
  • Over 12,500: "highly active"

This scale is a description, not a grade. The same researchers noted that 10,000 steps a day may not be realistic for some groups, including older adults and people living with chronic health conditions [4]. They suggested goals based on step-by-step increases from where a person starts instead [4].

A later review by an international group of experts found that healthy adults typically take anywhere from 4,000 to 18,000 steps a day [5]. That's a huge range, which is one more reason a one-size goal doesn't fit everyone.

How do you pick your own step goal?

Start from where you are, not from a number on a box.

Having a goal helps. A review of pedometer studies found that people using pedometers walked about 2,491 more steps a day than people who didn't, in the randomized trials [6]. Having a step goal was an important predictor of walking more [6]. The authors noted it wasn't yet clear whether those gains last over the long term [6].

Your goal also doesn't have to stay fixed. In a one-year trial of 512 adults who weren't active enough, some people got a daily goal that adjusted to how much they'd recently walked [7]. Others got the same fixed goal every day. The people with adjusting goals were more likely to get some brisk walking in on a given day [7]. That's one trial, but it supports the idea of a goal that moves with you.

In step-counting programs, people tend to add about 2,000 to 2,500 steps a day over time [5]. That's a useful sense of what a realistic increase looks like.

Do brisk steps count more?

Speed matters for meeting the official activity guidelines. The CDC says adults need at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, such as brisk walking, for example 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week [8].

In steps, about 100 steps a minute is a reasonable floor for moderate-intensity walking for most adults [5]. That means 30 minutes of brisk walking adds roughly 3,000 steps on top of what you already do [5]. The same review estimated that adults who get the minimum recommended activity in daily life take about 7,000 to 8,000 steps a day [5].

So a goal of 7,000 or more, with some of it brisk, lines up well with both the step research and the guidelines.

Do you need to hit your goal every day?

Probably not. A 2023 study followed about 3,100 U.S. adults for 10 years after they wore a step tracker for one week [9].

Compared with people who never hit 8,000 steps in a day, people who hit 8,000 or more on just 1 to 2 days a week had a 14.9 percentage point lower risk of dying during the study [9]. For 3 to 7 days a week, it was 16.5 points lower [9]. The benefit leveled off at about 3 days a week [9].

That's encouraging if your weekdays are packed. It's still an observational study, so it can't prove that the walking caused the difference. But it suggests your weekly pattern matters more than any one day.

Try this: set your step goal in 5 minutes

  1. Find your baseline. Look at your average daily steps over the past week or two in your phone's health app. That's your starting point.
  2. Add a small, specific amount. Set your goal about 1,000 steps above your average. At about 100 steps a minute, that's roughly 10 minutes of walking [5], like a short walk after lunch.
  3. Raise it when it feels easy. Once you hit your goal most days for a week or two, bump it up again.
  4. Aim for the range that fits you. Around 6,000 to 8,000 a day if you're 60 or older, and 8,000 to 10,000 if you're younger, is where the benefits in the big studies started to level off [2].
  5. Add some brisk minutes. Work toward 150 minutes a week of brisk walking [8]. Walking at a pace where you can talk but not sing is a good guide.
  6. Judge the week, not the day. A low day isn't a failure. Look at your weekly average instead.

If you already walk more than 10,000 steps a day, keep going. The 2025 review called 10,000 a fine target for people who are already active [1]. If you're starting from zero and the whole idea makes you groan, try our tips on how to start walking when you hate exercise.

When should you talk to your doctor first?

Walking is safe for most people. But check with your doctor before you raise your step goal a lot if:

  • You have heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, or another long-term condition.
  • You're pregnant.
  • You've fallen recently or feel unsteady on your feet.
  • You have joint pain or an injury that walking makes worse.
  • You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.

Your doctor can help you choose a safe starting goal. If heart health is your main reason for walking, see how many steps a day you need for heart health.

FAQ

Is 5,000 steps a day enough?

It's better than fewer. Benefits start well below that [3]. But 5,000 is the edge of what researchers call sedentary [4], so it's a good stepping stone, not a finish line.

Is 10,000 steps a day too many?

No. It's a fine goal if you're already active [1]. It's just not the only goal that counts.

Should my step goal change as I get older?

Big studies found the benefit leveled off at a lower count for adults 60 and older, around 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day [2].

Do steps from housework count?

Yes. The big studies used wearable devices that measured people's total daily steps [1][3].
