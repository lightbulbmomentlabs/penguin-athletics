If you hate exercise, you're not broken, and you're not alone. Lots of people dread the gym, the sweat, and the feeling of being bad at something.

The good news is that walking doesn't have to feel like exercise at all. It can be a short stroll after lunch or a lap around the block while you call a friend. The research on getting started is surprisingly kind to people who'd rather not.

Here's what the evidence says about why small amounts count, how habits form, and how to make walking something you might even like. There's a simple 4-week plan at the end. (For the full picture, see our [walking basics guide](/learn/walking-basics/).)

## Does a little bit of walking actually count?

Yes. The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines say clearly that some physical activity is better than none [2]. The CDC puts it the same way: adults who sit less and do any amount of moderate activity gain some health benefits [3].

The U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines add an encouraging detail. The people who move the least benefit the most from even modest increases in activity [1]. If you're starting from close to zero, every walk you add matters more for you than for someone who already exercises.

The long-term goal for adults is at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, like brisk walking [1][2]. That's about 30 minutes a day, five days a week [3]. But that's a destination, not a starting line.

### Do short walks count, or do I need 30 minutes?

Short walks count. A review done for the U.S. guidelines looked at whether activity had to last at least 10 minutes to be worth it [4]. It found that activity of any bout length was linked to better health, including a lower risk of dying early [4].

That evidence comes from observational studies, so it shows a link rather than proof [4]. Still, the CDC agrees you can break your activity into smaller chunks of time across the week [3]. A 5-minute walk is not a waste of time.

## Why should you start so small?

Starting tiny feels almost silly. That's the point. Health psychologists recommend small, manageable changes, because failing at a big goal can be discouraging [5].

In one example, they suggest a person who isn't active start by walking one or two extra bus stops, not the whole route [5]. They also note that simpler actions tend to become habits more quickly than complicated routines [5].

The CDC gives the same advice for anyone starting out: start slowly and work your way up to more time or more challenging activities [6]. You can add more later, once walking feels normal.

## How long does it take to make walking a habit?

Longer than the "21 days" you may have heard. That number didn't come from habit research [5].

In a study of people building a new daily health habit, like going for a walk, the behavior took an average of **66 days** to feel automatic [5]. There was a lot of variation between people and between habits [5]. So if week three still feels like effort, you're right on schedule.

### What makes a habit stick?

Habits form when you repeat an action in the same situation, until the situation itself starts to trigger the action [5]. Researchers call that situation a cue.

Their advice is simple: pick a time and place you run into every day, and do the action every time you get there [5]. A cue inside a routine you already have works well, like "after breakfast" or "when I get home from work" [5].

The CDC also suggests writing activity on your calendar so it becomes a regular part of your schedule [6].

## How can you make walking less miserable?

How you feel about walking matters more than you might think. A systematic review of 66 studies looked at what keeps people exercising [7].

Doing an activity because you find it satisfying or enjoyable is what researchers call intrinsic motivation [7]. That kind of motivation was more closely linked to sticking with exercise over the long term [7]. Most of those studies were observational, so they show a pattern rather than proof [7].

In other words, forcing yourself through something you hate is a weak long-term plan. Making walking more pleasant is a stronger one. The CDC recommends starting with activities, places, and times you enjoy, and trying activities with other people for motivation and encouragement [6].

A few ideas that people find help:

- **Save something good for the walk.** A favorite podcast, an audiobook, or a playlist you only listen to while walking gives you something to look forward to.
- **Pick a nicer route.** A park, a quiet street with trees, or a path by water can make the same 10 minutes feel shorter.
- **Bring company.** Walk with a friend, a partner, a coworker, or a dog. Or walk while you're on a phone call.
- **Make it about something else.** Walk to get coffee, to run an errand, or to look at holiday lights. The walk becomes the way there, not the point.

## Does tracking your steps really help?

It does seem to. A review in *JAMA* pooled 26 studies of people using pedometers (step counters) [8]. In the randomized trials, pedometer users walked about **2,491 more steps a day** than people who didn't use them [8].

Having a step goal was an important predictor of walking more [8]. The researchers noted it wasn't yet clear whether the boost lasts over the long term [8].

A larger review of 122 studies looked at which techniques help people be more active and eat better [9]. Self-monitoring, meaning keeping track of what you actually do, explained more of the difference between programs than any other technique [9]. Programs that paired self-monitoring with at least one closely related technique worked better than other programs [9].

So pick a small, realistic step goal, check your count at the end of the day, and nudge the goal up when it starts to feel easy. A phone or a step-tracking app can do the counting for you.

## Are rest days okay?

Yes, and missing a day isn't the end of the habit. In the habit study, missing one chance to do the behavior didn't seriously set people back, and progress picked up again soon after [5].

That means one skipped walk is just one skipped walk. The useful move is to show up at your usual cue the next day, without making up for it or starting over. If you need a full day off, plan it in, so it feels like part of the routine rather than a failure.

## A simple 4-week starter plan

This plan follows the "start slowly" idea [6] and builds a habit around one daily cue [5]. Adjust it to your life. Slower is fine.

1. **Week 1: Find your cue.** Walk for 5 to 10 minutes, at an easy pace, on at least 5 days. Do it at the same point in your day each time, like right after lunch.
2. **Week 2: Add a little time.** Walk 10 to 15 minutes on at least 5 days. Check your step count each evening, and note your daily average.
3. **Week 3: Set a step goal.** Aim for a few hundred steps a day above your Week 2 average. Walk at a pace where you can talk but feel a bit warmer.
4. **Week 4: Build toward 20 minutes.** Walk 15 to 20 minutes on at least 5 days. Two shorter walks in one day count too [3].

By the end of Week 4, you'll be at roughly 75 to 100 minutes a week. That's real progress toward the 150-minute goal [1]. Keep adding a few minutes every week or two from there.

If you're curious what you're building toward, read about [how many steps a day are linked to a longer life](/learn/how-many-steps-a-day-to-live-longer/). Walking can also help your [blood pressure](/learn/does-walking-lower-blood-pressure/).

## When should you talk to your doctor?

Walking is gentle, but check with your doctor before you start if any of these apply:

- You have heart disease, high blood pressure, or diabetes.
- You're pregnant or recently had a baby.
- You have an injury, joint pain, or a history of falls.
- You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you move.

The CDC suggests asking a health professional which activities are right for your age, fitness level, and health [6]. If you take medicine, keep taking it as prescribed. Walking works alongside medical care, not instead of it.

## FAQ

### What if I only have 5 minutes?

Take the 5-minute walk. Some activity is better than none [2], and activity in short bouts is linked to better health [4].

### Is walking enough exercise to count?

Yes. Brisk walking counts as moderate-intensity activity, the kind the guidelines recommend [1][3]. The guidelines also suggest muscle-strengthening activities on 2 or more days a week [1].

### What if I miss a few days?

Pick up again at your usual cue. In habit research, missing one opportunity didn't seriously set back habit formation [5].

### Should I aim for 10,000 steps right away?

No. Start from your current daily average and add a little at a time [6]. Having a step goal helps people walk more [8], but it should be one you can actually reach.
