Walking vs running: which is better for your heart?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 11, 2026 6 min read

The short answer

Both are good for your heart, and neither clearly wins. In a study of about 49,000 walkers and runners, burning the same amount of energy by walking or running led to similar drops in the risk of high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, and possibly heart disease. Running gets you there in less time, while walking takes longer but is easier to start and stick with.

Runners and walkers love to argue about who has it better. When it comes to your heart, the research is less dramatic than the debate.

Both activities are linked to a healthier heart. The bigger difference is time: running packs more effort into each minute, so you get to the same place faster.

Here's what the studies show, where running has an edge, where walking does, and how to pick the one you'll actually keep doing. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to walking and your heart.)

Is running better than walking for your heart?

Not if you compare them fairly. The best head-to-head study looked at about 33,000 runners and 16,000 walkers for about 6 years [1]. Instead of comparing minutes, it compared how much energy each person burned through exercise [1].

For the same amount of energy, both groups saw similar drops in risk [1]. For each extra daily "unit" of exercise energy, running was linked to a 4.2% lower risk of high blood pressure and walking to a 7.2% lower risk [1]. For high cholesterol, it was 4.3% for running and 7.0% for walking [1]. For diabetes, about 12% for both [1].

For coronary heart disease, each unit was linked to a 4.5% lower risk for running and 9.3% for walking [1]. The researchers found no significant difference between walking and running for blood pressure, diabetes, or heart disease [1]. Walking came out only slightly ahead for cholesterol [1].

Their conclusion: walking and running "produced similar risk reductions" for these heart risk factors, "and possibly" heart disease, when the energy burned was the same [1].

This was an observational study, and people reported their own diagnoses [1]. So it shows strong links, not proof that one activity caused the difference.

What does running do for your heart?

Running has a strong track record. A large study followed 55,137 adults for an average of 15 years [2]. Compared with people who didn't run, runners had a 45% lower risk of dying from heart disease [2].

What's striking is how little running it took. Even 5 to 10 minutes a day, at speeds slower than 6 miles per hour, was linked to lower risk [2]. People who kept running over the years had the biggest benefit, with a 50% lower risk of dying from heart disease than people who never ran [2].

A 2020 meta-analysis pooled 14 studies with more than 232,000 people [3]. Running was linked to a 30% lower risk of dying from heart disease compared with no running [3]. The researchers found no clear sign that running more often, longer, or faster added extra benefit [3].

A 2024 review of many sports found something similar: running was linked to a 27% lower risk of dying from heart disease, plus better fitness and heart function at rest [4].

What does walking do for your heart?

Walking has the edge on one kind of evidence: trials. Instead of just watching people, researchers assigned inactive adults to walk or not, then measured what changed.

A meta-analysis of 32 walking trials found that walking improved fitness and lowered systolic blood pressure (the top number) by about 3.6 points and diastolic (the bottom number) by about 1.5 points [5]. It also trimmed waist size and body fat a little [5]. It did not change cholesterol levels in these trials [5].

A second review of 37 walking trials found similar results for blood pressure, fasting blood sugar, and fitness [6]. It couldn't pin down exactly how fast, how long, or how often you need to walk to get those benefits [6].

Why does running save you time?

Running is harder per minute, so it counts for more per minute. The CDC lists brisk walking (2.5 miles per hour or faster) as moderate intensity, and jogging or running as vigorous [7].

The U.S. activity guidelines reflect that. Adults need 150 minutes a week of moderate activity like brisk walking, or 75 minutes a week of vigorous activity like running, or an equivalent mix of the two [8].

So running roughly halves the time it takes to hit the weekly target. Walking gets you to the same goal; it just asks for more minutes.

Not sure which one you're doing? Try the talk test. During moderate activity, you can talk but not sing [7]. During vigorous activity, you can't say more than a few words without pausing for breath [7].

Which one is easier on your body?

This is where walking often wins for beginners. Running injuries are common, especially early on.

A review of running studies found that new runners had about 17.8 injuries per 1,000 hours of running, compared with 7.7 for more experienced recreational runners [9]. The authors noted that studies defined injury differently, which makes exact numbers hard to compare [9].

We didn't find a matching number for walking. But walking is lower intensity [7], and an injury that keeps you on the couch for a month doesn't help your heart. If running hurts, walking is a solid way to keep the habit going.

So which should you choose?

The best one is the one you'll do most weeks. The research suggests your heart cares more about how much you move than which way you do it [1].

Running may suit you if:

  • You're short on time and want to meet the weekly goal in fewer minutes [8].
  • You already have a base of fitness and no joint issues.
  • You enjoy it. Even small amounts were linked to big benefits [2][3].

Walking may suit you if:

  • You're starting from little or no exercise.
  • You've had running injuries, or running just feels miserable.
  • You want something you can do daily, in regular clothes, with friends or on calls.

You can also mix them. The guidelines count a combination of moderate and vigorous activity [8].

How to get started (or switch it up)

  1. Start with walking. Aim for 30 minutes of brisk walking, 5 days a week [8]. Use the talk test to check your pace [7].
  2. Track the trend. Look at your weekly minutes or steps, not one bad day. See how many steps a day you need for heart health.
  3. Add short jogs if you want. Try jogging for a minute or two inside a walk, then walking again. Build up slowly.
  4. Go easy as a new runner. New runners get hurt more often [9]. Add a little at a time and take rest days.
  5. Keep going. People who kept running for years had the strongest results [2]. The same idea applies to walking: consistency beats intensity.

If exercise has never been your thing, start with how to start walking when you hate exercise.

When should you talk to your doctor?

Talk to your doctor first, especially before starting to run, if any of these apply:

  • You have heart disease, or you've had a heart attack or stroke.
  • You have high blood pressure, diabetes, or another long-term condition.
  • You take heart or blood pressure medicine.
  • You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.
  • You have joint problems, past injuries, or a higher risk of falls.

If you take medicine, keep taking it as prescribed. Exercise works alongside treatment, not instead of it.

FAQ

Is brisk walking as good as jogging?

For heart risk factors, it can be when you burn the same energy [1]. Walking just takes more time to get there [8].

How much running is enough for heart benefits?

Not much. Running 5 to 10 minutes a day, even slowly, was linked to a lower risk of dying from heart disease [2].

Is more running always better?

Not clearly. A meta-analysis found no clear extra benefit from running more often, longer, or faster [3].

Can walking lower cholesterol?

The evidence is mixed. In one head-to-head study, walkers had lower risk of high cholesterol [1], but walking trials didn't change cholesterol levels [5].

  • How many steps a day do you need for heart health?

    Heart · 6 min readYou probably don't need 10,000. In large studies, people who took more steps had a lower risk of heart disease, with most of the benefit showing up by about 7,000 to 9,000 steps a day. In adults 60 and older, 6,000 to 9,000 steps a day was linked to a 40% to 50% lower risk of heart disease than about 2,000 steps.

  • Does walking lower blood pressure?

    Heart · 6 min readYes. A large Cochrane review of 73 trials found that regular walking lowered systolic blood pressure (the top number) by about 4 mmHg on average. Most of the walking programs in those trials averaged around 150 minutes of moderate walking a week for a few months.

  • How to start walking when you hate exercise

    Basics · 7 min readStart smaller than you think you need to, like a 5 to 10 minute walk tied to something you already do every day, and add a few minutes each week. Health guidelines say some activity is better than none and short walks count. New habits took about 66 days on average to feel automatic in one study, so aim for consistency, not intensity.

  • How many steps a day to live longer?

    Longevity · 7 min readIn a pooled analysis of 15 studies, the risk of dying early kept falling as steps rose, then leveled off at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for adults 60 and older and 8,000 to 10,000 for younger adults. You don't need 10,000 to benefit: a 2025 review linked 7,000 steps a day to a 47% lower risk of death than 2,000 steps.
