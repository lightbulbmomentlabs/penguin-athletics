Almost every step counter nudges you toward 10,000 a day. It feels official, like a doctor somewhere picked it after years of careful study.

Nobody did. The number came first, and the research came decades later. The good news is that the research mostly backs up the idea behind it: more steps are linked to better health, and you get a lot of the benefit well before 10,000.

Here's where the number came from, what the big studies actually found, and how to pick a goal that works for you. (For the full picture on walking and lifespan, see our guide to [walking and longevity](/learn/walking-longevity/).)

## Where did the 10,000 steps rule come from?

It came from advertising, not a lab. A 2022 analysis in *The Lancet Public Health* put it plainly: the 10,000-step goal "is not based on evidence, but instead originates from a marketing campaign in Japan" [1].

Researchers who studied older women made the same point. They wrote that 10,000 steps a day is commonly believed to be necessary for health, but the number "has limited scientific basis" [2].

That doesn't make the number wrong. It just means it was never tested before it became the default goal on millions of devices.

## What do studies say about 10,000 steps?

Over the past decade, researchers have finally tested it. They gave thousands of people step-counting devices, then followed them for years to see who stayed healthy.

These are observational studies. They show that people who walk more tend to have better health, not proof that the steps alone caused it. Researchers adjust for other factors, but some may remain [3].

### Risk of early death

This is the best-studied outcome, and the pattern is consistent. Risk drops steeply as people go from very few steps to a moderate amount, then it flattens.

- A 2023 review of 12 studies and more than 111,000 people found a lower risk of death starting at about **2,500 steps a day**, compared with 2,000 [4]. The biggest drop, about **60% lower risk**, showed up at around 8,800 steps a day [4].
- A pooled analysis of 15 studies and about 47,000 adults found risk leveled off at about **6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for adults 60 and older**, and **8,000 to 10,000 for adults under 60** [1]. For both men and women, the lowest risk showed up at about 7,000 to 9,000 steps a day [1].
- In about 16,700 older women with an average age of 72, death rates were lower at about 4,400 steps a day than at 2,700, and they leveled off around **7,500 steps** [2].

A 2025 review of 57 studies found that, compared with 2,000 steps a day, **7,000 steps was linked to a 47% lower risk of death** from any cause [3]. For most adults, that's the zone where the curve starts to bend.

### Heart disease

The heart results look similar. The 2023 review linked about 7,100 steps a day to the biggest drop in heart disease risk, about 51% lower than 2,000 steps [4]. The 2025 review linked 7,000 steps to a 25% lower risk of developing heart disease [3].

For a deeper look, see [how many steps a day you need for heart health](/learn/how-many-steps-a-day-do-you-need-for-heart-health/).

### Dementia, diabetes, and mood

This is where 10,000 holds up best. A UK study of about 78,400 adults aged 40 to 79 found the biggest drop in dementia risk, about 51%, at around **9,800 steps a day** [5]. Even about 3,800 steps a day was linked to a 25% lower risk [5]. The 2025 review, which pooled fewer dementia studies, saw that curve start to bend sooner, at around 5,000 to 7,000 steps [3].

The 2025 review found that for type 2 diabetes and depressive symptoms, risk kept dropping in a straight line as steps went up [3]. There was no clear point where more steps stopped helping.

## So is 10,000 steps a myth?

Not quite. It's better to think of it as one good target, not the only one.

The 2025 review summed it up well: 10,000 steps can still be a good target for people who are more active, but 7,000 steps a day is linked to meaningful health gains and "might be a more realistic and achievable target for some" [3].

Two things are clear from the research:

- **You don't need 10,000 to benefit.** Gains show up at a few thousand steps a day [4], and the biggest ones come from moving off the very bottom.
- **Going past 10,000 isn't wasted.** For type 2 diabetes and depressive symptoms, more steps kept helping [3]. One large study linked the lowest dementia risk to about 9,800 steps [5].

As of 2022, one research group noted there were no evidence-based public health guidelines that set a daily step number [1].

## Do step goals help, even if the number is made up?

They seem to. A review of 26 pedometer studies found that people using pedometers walked about **2,500 more steps a day** than people who didn't, in the randomized trials [6]. Having a step goal, such as 10,000 steps a day, was an important predictor of becoming more active [6].

Pedometer users in those studies also saw small drops in body mass index and blood pressure [6]. The authors noted it wasn't known whether these changes last over the long term [6].

So the number itself may matter less than having one. A clear target gives you something to aim at.

## How do steps compare with the official guidelines?

The CDC's guidelines for adults are written in minutes, not steps. Adults need at least **150 minutes a week of moderate activity**, such as 30 minutes a day, 5 days a week, plus muscle-strengthening activity at least 2 days a week [7]. The CDC also notes that some activity is better than none [7].

A review that translated those minutes into steps found that walking at about **100 steps a minute** is a reasonable floor for moderate intensity [8]. Thirty minutes at that pace adds up to about 3,000 steps, on top of your usual daily movement [8]. The same review estimated that meeting the minimum guideline works out to roughly **7,000 to 8,000 steps a day** [8].

That lines up nicely with where the big studies see risk start to level off [1][3].

## How to pick your own step goal

Forget the one-size-fits-all number. Try this instead:

1. **Check your real baseline.** Look at your average steps over the past week, not just today. That's where you start.
2. **Add 1,000 to 2,000 steps a day.** Studies of step-counting programs typically see gains of about 2,000 to 2,500 steps a day [8]. That's a 10- to 20-minute walk for most people.
3. **Aim for 7,000 first.** It's linked to big health gains in the 2025 review [3], and it's reachable for most people.
4. **Go higher if you want to.** Adults under 60 kept seeing benefits up to about 8,000 to 10,000 steps a day [1]. If 10,000 feels good, keep it.
5. **Make some of it brisk.** About 100 steps a minute counts as moderate walking [8], which helps you meet the CDC's 150 minutes a week [7].
6. **Judge the week, not the day.** One low day doesn't erase anything. The habit is what counts.

## When should you talk to your doctor?

Walking is safe for most people. But check with your doctor before raising your step count a lot if:

- You have heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, or another long-term condition.
- You're pregnant, or recovering from an injury or surgery.
- You've fallen recently or feel unsteady on your feet.
- You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.

Your doctor can help you set a safe starting point and a pace for building up.

## FAQ

### Is 5,000 steps a day enough?

It's a lot better than very few. In older women, about 4,400 steps a day was linked to lower death rates than 2,700 [2]. But more steps are linked to more benefit, so it's a good place to build from.

### Is 10,000 steps too much for older adults?

Not if you can do it comfortably. Adults 60 and older saw risk level off at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day [1], so that range is a realistic goal.

### Does walking speed matter more than steps?

The evidence is mixed. In older women, faster stepping wasn't clearly linked to lower death rates once total steps were counted [2]. A larger review found a faster pace was linked to extra benefit [4].

### Do steps from chores and errands count?

Yes. The step studies used devices that counted all steps, not just planned walks [1][5]. Steps at work and around the house add up.
