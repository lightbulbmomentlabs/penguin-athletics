Most of us never think about how we walk. We just go. But a few small changes to how you hold your head, shoulders, and arms can make walks feel easier and more comfortable, especially longer ones.

The good news: this isn't about a stiff, military-style posture. Researchers who study back pain have found no single "correct" posture that everyone needs to hold. What helps is a relaxed, upright stance and letting your body move the way it's built to.

Here's what health experts recommend, what the research says about arm swing, phones, and mood, and a simple checklist you can try on your next walk. (New to walking? Start with our [walking basics guide](/learn/walking-basics/).)

## What does good walking posture look like?

Harvard Health and Mayo Clinic give very similar advice [1][2]. Start at the top and work down:

- **Stand tall.** Imagine being gently lifted from the crown of your head [1]. Keep your back straight, not arched forward or leaning backward [2].
- **Eyes up.** Look forward, not down [2]. Harvard suggests aiming your gaze about **10 to 20 feet** in front of you, close enough to spot obstacles [1].
- **Relax your shoulders.** Roll them up, back, and down, then keep them away from your ears [1]. Mayo Clinic says to relax your neck, shoulders, and back [2].
- **Let your arms swing.** Swing from the shoulders, not the elbows, like a pendulum [1]. A slight bend in the elbows is fine, and pumping your arms a little is OK [2].
- **Keep your core lightly engaged.** Tighten your stomach muscles a little [2], without tucking your tailbone under or sticking your belly out [1].
- **Step smoothly.** Roll each foot from heel to toe [1][2].

That's it. You don't need to hold all six in your head at once. Pick one or two per walk.

## Is there one "correct" posture?

Not really, and that should take some pressure off. A large review of 41 systematic reviews looked at whether specific spinal postures, standing, bending, or twisting cause low back pain [3].

The authors found both positive and null links, and concluded there is **no consensus** that these physical exposures cause low back pain [3]. Some links have been documented, but they don't explain what causes back pain [3].

So think of posture cues as ways to feel more comfortable and move more freely, not as rules that protect you from injury. If a cue feels forced or painful, ease off. Comfortable and relaxed beats rigid and "perfect".

## Why should you swing your arms?

It looks like arm swing doesn't do much. It turns out it saves energy.

In a lab study, researchers measured 10 people walking with and without normal arm swing [4]. Normal arm swinging took very little shoulder effort, while holding the arms still on purpose required **12% more metabolic energy** [4].

The researchers also tested walking with the "wrong" arm pattern, swinging each arm with the leg on the same side. That raised energy use by **26%** [4]. Without arm swing, a twisting force from the ground on the body went up by 63% [4]. Your arms swing mostly on their own, and they make walking more efficient overall [4].

This was a small study, but the takeaway is simple: don't walk with your hands jammed in your pockets or your arms locked at your sides if you can help it. If you're carrying things, a backpack keeps your arms free.

## What does looking at your phone do to your walk?

It changes how you walk, and not in a good way.

In a study of 26 healthy adults, people walked in a straight line while not using a phone, reading on a phone, or typing on one [5]. When reading or texting, they walked slower, drifted more side to side, and held their head bent forward [5]. Texting had a bigger effect than reading [5].

A 2023 review pooled 22 studies with 592 people [6]. Using a phone while walking slowed people down and shortened their steps overall [6]. Texting and reading slowed walking speed, while calling and talking didn't seem to [6].

The researchers behind the first study warned that texting or reading while walking may add risk for pedestrians crossing the road or dealing with obstacles [5]. If you need to check something, step aside and stop for a moment.

## Can your posture affect your mood?

Maybe a little. The research here is early, but interesting.

In one study, people in a sad mood and people with depression walked differently from others [7]. They walked more slowly, swung their arms less, had less up-and-down head movement, and had a more slumped posture [7].

A later experiment flipped it around. Researchers used feedback to nudge 39 students into walking either in a "depressed" style or a "happy" style, at the same speed [8]. Later, the gap between positive and negative words they remembered was much smaller in the depressed-style walkers than in the happy-style walkers [8]. In other words, the depressed style tilted memory toward the negative.

These were small studies in students, not people being treated for depression [8], so they don't prove that standing tall will lift your mood. But walking tall with a relaxed arm swing costs nothing to try. ## Try this: a 1-minute posture check

Run through this list at the start of your walk, then once or twice along the way:

1. **Crown up.** Grow tall, as if a string is lifting the top of your head [1].
2. **Chin level, eyes ahead.** Look out 10 to 20 feet, not at your shoes [1].
3. **Shoulders down.** Shrug up, roll back, let them drop [1].
4. **Arms loose.** Let them swing from the shoulders, elbows slightly bent [1][2].
5. **Belly lightly firm.** Not sucked in, just engaged [2].
6. **Quiet feet.** Land on your heel and roll through to your toes [1]. Don't reach your leg far out in front of you, since Harvard notes that overstriding adds impact on your joints and slows you down [1].
7. **Phone away.** Or at least stop walking while you use it [5].

If you track your steps, a posture check is an easy thing to pair with each walk. If you're just getting into the habit, our tips on [how to start walking when you hate exercise](/learn/how-to-start-walking-when-you-hate-exercise/) can help.

## When should you talk to your doctor first?

Talk to your doctor or a physical therapist before changing how you walk if you have back, hip, knee, or foot pain, a recent injury, or a condition that affects your balance.

This matters most for older adults. A review of 19 studies found that a forward-rounded upper back (called hyperkyphosis) in adults 60 and older was linked to worse balance and a higher risk of falls [9]. Those studies were observational, so they show a link, not cause and effect [9].

The National Institute on Aging notes that problems with balance and walking are risk factors for falls [10]. It recommends telling your doctor about any fall, even one that didn't hurt, and says a physical or occupational therapist can help you choose and use a cane or walker safely [10]. If you use one, posture cues should fit around it, not replace it.

## FAQ

### Should I walk heel first?

For everyday walking, yes. Both Harvard Health and Mayo Clinic suggest rolling from heel to toe in a smooth motion [1][2].

### How far ahead should I look?

About 10 to 20 feet ahead, according to Harvard Health [1]. That keeps your head up while still letting you see what's coming.

### Is it bad to walk with hands in pockets?

It isn't dangerous, but it costs you a bit. Holding the arms still took about 12% more energy in one small lab study [4].

### Can bad posture cause back pain?

The evidence is mixed. A large review found no consensus that specific postures cause low back pain [3]. If walking hurts, talk to your doctor.
