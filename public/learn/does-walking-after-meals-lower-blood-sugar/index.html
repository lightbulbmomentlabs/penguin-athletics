Does walking after meals lower blood sugar?

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 16, 2026 6 min read

The short answer

Yes. Trials show that walking soon after you eat lowers the blood sugar rise that follows a meal. In one trial of adults with type 2 diabetes, a 10-minute walk after each main meal lowered after-meal blood sugar about 12% more than a single daily 30-minute walk, and about 22% more after dinner.

After you eat, your blood sugar goes up. That's normal. What you do in the hour or so after the meal can change how high it goes and how long it stays there.

The after-dinner walk is an old habit in a lot of families. Researchers have now tested it in trials, and the results are pretty consistent: a short, easy walk soon after eating flattens the rise.

Here's what the studies found, how long and how soon to walk, and who should check with a doctor first. (For the bigger picture on blood sugar, weight, and walking, see our guide to walking for weight loss.)

What does a walk after eating do to your blood sugar?

When you walk, your leg muscles need fuel, and blood sugar is one of the easiest fuels to grab. During aerobic exercise, muscles can take up as much as five times more glucose, and they can do it without needing extra insulin [7]. That's sugar that leaves your blood instead of hanging around in it.

The best test of this in real life comes from a trial in New Zealand. Forty-one adults with type 2 diabetes tried two walking plans for two weeks each [1]. In one, they walked 30 minutes a day whenever they liked. In the other, they walked 10 minutes after each main meal [1]. Total walking time was the same.

Continuous glucose monitors tracked their blood sugar. In the three hours after meals, the rise was about 12% lower with the after-meal walks [1]. After the evening meal, when people ate the most carbohydrate and sat the most, it was about 22% lower [1].

Is it better to walk before or after eating?

After. A 2023 meta-analysis pooled eight randomized trials with 116 people, 47 with type 2 diabetes and 69 without [2]. Exercise after a meal lowered the blood sugar rise compared with exercise before the meal and compared with not exercising at all [2].

Exercise before eating didn't lower after-meal blood sugar much compared with doing nothing [2]. The authors concluded that walking has a bigger effect when you do it "as soon as possible after a meal" [2].

One honest caveat: the review rated the trials as having a high risk of bias, and they were small [2]. The direction of the results is consistent, but the exact size of the effect is less certain.

How soon after a meal should you walk?

Sooner seems better. In the same meta-analysis, the longer people waited between eating and exercising, the smaller the drop in blood sugar [2].

The trials don't give a single perfect minute to start. In practice, that means heading out once you've finished eating rather than waiting an hour or two. If walking on a full stomach feels uncomfortable, start slow and pick up the pace as you go.

How long do you need to walk?

Not long. The main trial used 10 minutes after each meal [1]. A small study in 10 inactive adults aged 60 and older found that three 15-minute walks after meals improved blood sugar over the whole day [3]. Those after-meal walks were also better than one 45-minute walk at lowering blood sugar in the three hours after dinner [3].

Even shorter bursts help. In a lab trial of 19 adults aged 45 to 65 with higher body weight, people sat for most of the day after a sugary drink [4]. When they got up for 2 minutes of light walking every 20 minutes, the blood sugar rise was about a quarter lower than when they sat the whole time (5.2 vs 6.9 on the study's measure) [4]. Insulin went down too [4].

The American Diabetes Association's position statement sums it up: even brief bouts of 3 to 15 minutes of activity help lower blood sugar after meals, in people with prediabetes, type 1 diabetes, and type 2 diabetes [7].

Does the pace matter?

Easy walking works. In the 2-minute walking-break trial, light and moderate walking lowered blood sugar by similar amounts [4].

What matters more is moving instead of sitting. A 2022 meta-analysis of seven trials found that light walking breaks lowered after-meal blood sugar and insulin compared with sitting [5]. Walking also beat standing breaks [5].

A larger 2026 meta-analysis of 39 trials came to a similar answer. Of the types of activity breaks studied, walking had the biggest effect on blood sugar and insulin after meals [6]. Breaking up sitting every 15 to 20 minutes gave the largest drop in blood sugar [6].

So you don't need to power-walk. A stroll around the block counts.

Does it help if you don't have diabetes?

The evidence says yes, at least in the short term. The 2023 meta-analysis included more people without diabetes (69) than with it (47), and walking after meals still came out ahead [2]. The sitting-break trials studied general groups of adults, mostly people with higher body weight [4][5].

What we know less about is the long run. Most of these studies lasted a single day or a couple of weeks. The ADA notes that the longer-term health effects of breaking up sitting time are still being studied [7]. Regular walking overall does help: the ADA says aerobic exercise improves blood sugar control in type 2 diabetes, especially at 150 minutes a week or more [7].

A walk after meals is a good way to get some of those minutes in. For how walking affects weight, see can you lose weight just by walking?

How to start walking after meals

  1. Start with your biggest meal. For many people, that's dinner, where the trial saw the largest effect [1].
  2. Go within a few minutes of finishing. Sooner seems to work better than later [2].
  3. Aim for 10 minutes. That's what the main trial used [1]. If 10 feels like a lot, start with 5.
  4. Keep it easy. A comfortable pace is enough [4][5].
  5. Stuck at a desk or on a long call? Stand up and walk for a couple of minutes every 20 to 30 minutes [4][7].
  6. Count it toward your week. Most adults should aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and you don't have to do 30 minutes all at once [8]. Three 10-minute walks after meals add up to the same.
  7. Make the walk the cue. "Dishes in the sink, shoes on." Our guide on how to start walking when you hate exercise has more tricks like this.

Talk to your doctor first if...

If you have diabetes, talk to your doctor or diabetes care team before you add after-meal walks, especially if you take insulin or a sulfonylurea. These medicines can make blood sugar drop too low during exercise [8]. Low blood sugar during exercise is common in type 1 diabetes, and it happens less often in type 2 diabetes in people who use insulin or medicines that make the body release more insulin [7].

Doing more activity than usual can lower blood sugar for up to 24 hours afterward [9]. Your care team may want you to check your blood sugar around your walks. Don't change your medicine or doses on your own.

Check with your doctor first, too, if you have heart disease, high blood pressure, nerve damage in your feet, are pregnant, or have a higher risk of falls, especially for walks after dark.

FAQ

Is a walk after breakfast as good as after dinner?

Any main meal is a fine place to start. In the trial, the biggest drop came after dinner, when people ate the most carbohydrate [1].

Can I just stand instead of walking?

Standing helps a little, but light walking breaks lowered blood sugar and insulin more than standing breaks did [5].

Is it bad to lie down right after eating?

The trials compared walking with sitting, not lying down. Sitting still after a meal led to a bigger blood sugar rise than short walks did [4][5].

Will walking after meals fix high blood sugar?

No single habit does that. It lowers the rise after meals, and it works best alongside regular activity, eating changes, and any treatment your doctor recommends [7].

    Weight · 6 min readA 30-minute brisk walk uses roughly 140 to 240 calories for most adults, and in one study, walking a mile used about 90 to 110 calories. The biggest factors are your body weight, your pace, and whether you're going uphill. Every calorie number, including the one on your watch, is an estimate, so treat it as a rough guide.

  • Can you lose weight just by walking?

    Weight · 6 min readYes, but usually only a little. In trials of walking alone, people lost about 1.4 kg (roughly 3 pounds) on average, and programs that combine walking with eating changes lead to more weight loss. Walking still improves blood pressure, blood sugar, and fitness even when the scale barely moves.

  • Does walking help you sleep better?

    Mind · 6 min readYes, for many people it does. A 2026 review of 21 trials found that regular walking improved self-reported sleep quality in adults, with the biggest gains in people who slept worst to start with. The certainty is still low, and walking works best alongside proven insomnia treatment, not instead of it.
