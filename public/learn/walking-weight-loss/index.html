Walking for weight loss: the complete guide

By the Penguin Athletics editorial team Published November 6, 2026 11 min read

The short answer

Walking on its own usually leads to modest weight loss. In a review of pedometer-based walking programs with no diet changes, people lost about 1.3 kg (roughly 3 pounds) over a median of 16 weeks. Walking does more when paired with changes in eating, and it helps prevent weight gain and improves blood sugar and insulin sensitivity whatever the scale says.

Walking is the most common way people try to get more active, and a lot of them start because they want to change their weight. That's a fair goal. But the research tells a more interesting story than the number on a scale.

Walking on its own usually moves body weight a little, not a lot. Where it really shines is in what it does for your blood sugar, your insulin, your heart, and your ability to keep your weight steady over the years. Those benefits show up whether or not your weight changes.

This guide covers what the studies actually show: how much weight walking tends to change, what affects how much energy you use, how walking after meals helps blood sugar, what it does for diabetes risk and the fat around your organs, whether it makes you hungrier, and how to start.

What does walking really do for your health?

Before we get to the scale, here's the big picture. The U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines say the health benefits of physical activity are generally independent of body weight [1]. In plain terms, regular activity is good for you no matter how your weight changes over time [1].

Some benefits start almost right away. The guidelines list improvements in insulin sensitivity, blood pressure, sleep, and anxiety that can show up soon after activity [1]. Others, like better fitness and a lasting drop in blood pressure, build over weeks to months [1].

So if you walk for months and your weight barely moves, the walking has not failed. It has very likely been working on things you can't see in the mirror. (For more on the heart side, see our guide to walking and your heart.)

How much weight can you lose just by walking?

Usually a modest amount. It's worth knowing that up front so you can set expectations that keep you going instead of letting you down.

A meta-analysis pulled together nine studies of pedometer-based walking programs that did not include any diet changes [2]. The 307 participants walked for anywhere from 4 weeks to a year, with a median of 16 weeks [2]. On average, they lost 1.27 kg, or about 3 pounds [2]. That works out to roughly 0.05 kg a week [2].

The same review found that longer programs led to more weight loss than shorter ones [2]. Walking is a slow, steady tool, not a quick one.

A larger review of 32 randomized trials in inactive adults found a similar result. Walking programs, with walking as the only change, lowered body weight by about 1.37 kg compared with not walking [3]. They also trimmed waist size by about 1.5 cm and body fat by about 1.2 percentage points [3]. Those same walking programs improved fitness and lowered blood pressure, too [3].

Those numbers are averages. Some people lose more and some lose none. We cover this question in more depth in Can you lose weight just by walking?

Does more walking mean more weight loss?

Generally, yes, but it takes a lot. The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) reviewed the evidence and concluded that 150 to 250 minutes a week of moderate activity provides only modest weight loss [4]. Larger amounts, above 250 minutes a week, have been linked to clinically meaningful weight loss [4].

The U.S. guidelines say something similar. People who want to lose more than 5% of their body weight may need more than 300 minutes a week of moderate activity [1]. That's about an hour a day, five days a week.

People also vary a great deal in how much activity they need [1]. Your body, your schedule, and your eating all play a part.

Why does walking work better alongside changes in eating?

Because both sides of the energy balance matter. The U.S. guidelines say physical activity and calorie intake both have to be considered, and that combining the two tends to work better for weight loss than either one alone [1].

The ACSM found the same thing. Moderate activity of 150 to 250 minutes a week improved weight loss in studies that also used moderate diet changes, but not in studies with severe diet restriction [4].

The CDC puts it simply: to lose weight and keep it off, you'll need a high amount of physical activity unless you also adjust what you eat [5]. Reaching and keeping a healthy weight takes both regular activity and healthy eating patterns [5].

We won't give you a diet plan here. If you want to change how you eat, a doctor or a registered dietitian can help you find an approach that fits your health and your life.

Can walking help you keep weight steady?

This is where walking may matter most. For many adults, the bigger challenge isn't losing weight. It's not slowly gaining it over the years, or keeping it off after a change.

A systematic review done for the U.S. guidelines found strong evidence that physical activity is linked to preventing weight gain in adults [6]. Most of that evidence comes from long-term observational studies, so it shows a link rather than proof of cause [6]. Based on more limited evidence, the effect looked strongest above 150 minutes a week of moderate-to-vigorous activity [6].

The guidelines also note that many people need more than 150 minutes a week of moderate activity just to keep their weight stable [1]. People trying to keep off weight they've lost may need more than 300 minutes a week [1]. The CDC adds that the exact amount varies greatly from person to person [5].

The ACSM review found that, in observational studies, people kept weight off more successfully when they did more than 250 minutes of activity a week after weight loss [4]. It also noted that well-designed randomized trials on this question are still missing [4]. So the evidence is promising, but not final.

The practical takeaway: walking is a habit you keep for the long run, not a short-term fix. The people who seem to benefit most are the ones who keep doing it.

How many calories does walking burn?

It depends mostly on three things: your body weight, how fast you walk, and whether you're going uphill. We'll skip exact calorie numbers, because they vary so much from person to person. Here's how the pieces fit together instead.

Scientists measure activity using METs (metabolic equivalents). One MET is a standard estimate of the energy your body uses at rest [7]. An activity rated at 4 METs uses about four times that resting energy.

Pace

The 2024 Compendium of Physical Activities, the main reference researchers use, rates walking like this on a firm, level surface [8]:

  • 2.5 mph (an easy stroll): 3.0 METs
  • 2.8 to 3.4 mph (moderate pace): 3.8 METs
  • 3.5 to 3.9 mph (brisk, walking for exercise): 4.8 METs
  • 4.0 to 4.4 mph (very brisk): 5.5 METs

So picking up your pace from a stroll to a brisk walk uses noticeably more energy in the same amount of time.

Hills and incline

Grade makes a big difference. Walking uphill at a moderate-to-brisk pace on a 1% to 5% grade is rated at 5.3 METs [8]. On a 6% to 10% grade, it rises to 7.0 METs [8]. A hilly route or a treadmill incline is an easy way to make the same walk harder.

Body weight and other factors

The standard MET is expressed per kilogram of body weight [7]. That means the same walk at the same pace uses more total energy for a person with a higher body weight than for a person with a lower one.

Researchers also note that resting energy use differs with age, sex, height, body weight, and body makeup [7]. That's why any calorie number from an app, a treadmill, or a chart is an estimate, not an exact count. Use it as a rough guide, not a target.

How does walking after meals help your blood sugar?

This is one of the most practical findings in walking research. When you eat, your blood sugar rises. Moving your muscles soon afterward helps pull some of that sugar out of your blood.

In one randomized crossover trial, 41 adults with type 2 diabetes tried two kinds of walking advice for two weeks each [9]. One was to walk 30 minutes once a day. The other was to walk 10 minutes after each main meal [9]. Both added up to the same 30 minutes.

Blood sugar after meals was about 12% lower with the after-meal walks [9]. The effect was biggest after the evening meal, when people tended to eat the most carbohydrate and sit the most [9]. That's one trial, so it's a promising result rather than a final word.

A meta-analysis of short, one-day trials looked at breaking up long periods of sitting [10]. Short, light walking breaks lowered blood sugar and insulin after meals compared with sitting the whole time [10]. Light walking breaks also worked better than standing breaks [10].

The appeal here is that it doesn't take much. A gentle stroll after dinner counts. You don't have to be sweaty or out of breath.

Can walking lower your risk of type 2 diabetes?

The evidence here is encouraging. Walking seems to help both by improving how your body handles insulin and, for some people, through modest changes in weight.

Insulin sensitivity

Insulin is the hormone that helps move sugar from your blood into your cells. When your body responds well to insulin, that's called good insulin sensitivity. The U.S. guidelines say insulin sensitivity can improve after just a single session of physical activity [1]. For people who already have type 2 diabetes, physical activity helps control blood sugar [1].

Diabetes risk

According to the U.S. guidelines, regular physical activity strongly reduces the risk of developing type 2 diabetes in people of all body sizes [1]. Those benefits start at levels of activity below 150 minutes a week, and more activity seems to lower risk further [1].

A systematic review of 10 long-term studies, with more than 300,000 participants, looked at moderate activity and diabetes [11]. People who regularly walked, typically 2.5 hours a week or more of brisk walking, had about a 30% lower risk of type 2 diabetes than people who did almost no walking [11]. The link held even after accounting for body mass index [11]. These were observational studies, so they show a strong link, not proof that walking alone caused the difference.

The strongest trial evidence comes from the Diabetes Prevention Program. It included 3,234 adults with higher-than-normal blood sugar [12]. One group was coached on lifestyle changes, with goals of at least 7% weight loss and at least 150 minutes of physical activity a week [12]. Over about 2.8 years, that program lowered the rate of new type 2 diabetes by 58% compared with placebo [12]. Activity was one part of a package that also included changes in eating, so the result reflects the whole program, not walking alone.

Talk to your doctor first if you have diabetes or prediabetes

Walking is safe for most people, but talk to your doctor before starting a new routine if you:

  • Have type 2 or type 1 diabetes, or have been told you have prediabetes.
  • Take insulin or other medicines that lower blood sugar.
  • Have heart disease or another long-term health condition.
  • Get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.

Keep taking any medicine as prescribed. Walking works alongside your treatment, not instead of it. If your numbers improve over time, your doctor can decide whether anything should change.

Does walking help with visceral fat?

Visceral fat is the fat stored deep in your belly, around your organs. It matters for health more than fat stored elsewhere, because it's closely tied to metabolic problems.

A meta-analysis of 35 randomized trials measured visceral fat with CT or MRI scans [13]. Aerobic exercise reduced visceral fat compared with no exercise [13]. Resistance training on its own did not show a significant effect [13]. The authors suggested that aerobic exercise, even below current recommendations for weight management, may be enough to improve visceral fat [13].

Those trials looked at aerobic exercise in general, not walking alone. Still, walking is the most common form of aerobic exercise, and the U.S. guidelines note that physical activity helps reduce abdominal fat and preserve muscle during weight loss [1].

This is a good example of why the scale doesn't tell the whole story. Changes in the fat around your organs can matter for your health even when your total weight barely moves.

Does walking make you hungrier?

It's a common worry: will a walk just make you eat more and cancel it out? The research suggests that, for most people, it doesn't work that way in the short term.

A review of exercise and appetite found that single bouts of exercise create a short-term energy gap without triggering a matching rise in appetite [14]. More limited evidence suggests regular exercise may even help people feel more satisfied after meals [14].

But the same review stressed that responses vary a lot between individuals [14]. Some people do feel hungrier after activity. If that's you, it's not a failure. It's useful information about your body, and a doctor or dietitian can help you work with it.

How to start walking for your weight and health

You don't need a gym, a plan from a trainer, or special gear. Start small and build. If you're starting from zero, our walking basics guide covers shoes, pace, and form. Try this:

  1. Start with what you can do today. A short walk is a real start. The U.S. guidelines say some physical activity is better than none [1].
  2. Build toward 150 minutes a week. That's about 30 minutes a day, five days a week, at a brisk pace [5]. Split it into shorter walks if that's easier.
  3. Add a short walk after meals. Ten minutes after your biggest meal is a simple habit with a real effect on blood sugar [9].
  4. Pick up the pace or find a hill. Faster walking and inclines both raise the energy you use [8]. Change one thing at a time.
  5. Think in months, not weeks. Longer walking programs led to more weight change than shorter ones [2]. Give yourself time.
  6. Track health wins, not just weight. Notice your energy, sleep, mood, step count, and how hills feel. If you have them, look at blood pressure and blood sugar readings with your doctor.
  7. Pair it with eating changes if weight is your goal. Walking plus changes in eating tends to work better than either alone [1]. Get personal advice from a doctor or dietitian.

FAQ

How long until walking changes my weight?

Slowly. In walking-only programs, the average loss was about 0.05 kg a week, and longer programs led to more change [2]. Health benefits like better insulin sensitivity can start much sooner [1].

Does walking faster help more?

Faster walking uses more energy per minute than a slow stroll [8]. But the best pace is one you can keep up week after week.

Do I need 10,000 steps a day?

The U.S. guidelines set targets in minutes, not steps: at least 150 a week of moderate activity [1]. They also say some activity is better than none [1].

Should I walk before or after eating?

For blood sugar, after meals looks helpful. A 10-minute walk after each main meal lowered after-meal blood sugar in adults with type 2 diabetes [9].
