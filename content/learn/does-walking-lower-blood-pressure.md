Walking is one of the simplest things you can do for your blood pressure, and the research behind it is solid. It won't replace medicine if you need it, but it can move your numbers in the right direction.

Here's what the studies actually show, how much walking it takes, and how to start without overthinking it. (For the bigger picture, see our guide to [walking and your heart](/learn/walking-heart-health/).)

## How much does walking lower blood pressure?

The best summary we have is a 2021 Cochrane review. Cochrane reviews pool the results of many controlled trials, so they carry a lot of weight. This one included 73 trials and more than 5,700 people in 22 countries [1].

On average, walking lowered systolic blood pressure (the top number) by about **4.1 mmHg** compared with not walking [1]. The researchers rated this as moderate-certainty evidence, which means walking *probably* lowers it [1].

The bottom number, diastolic pressure, dropped by about **1.8 mmHg** [1]. Resting heart rate also went down by close to 3 beats per minute [1]. The evidence for those two was lower certainty, so treat them as likely, not guaranteed.

An earlier review of walking-only trials in inactive adults found something very similar. Systolic pressure fell by about 3.6 mmHg and diastolic by about 1.5 mmHg [2]. Two separate reviews landing in the same place is a good sign.

### Is 4 points really a big deal?

It might not sound like much. But small drops in blood pressure add up across a whole life.

A huge analysis of blood pressure medicine trials found that every 5 mmHg drop in systolic pressure was linked to about a **10% lower risk** of major heart events, like heart attack and stroke [4]. That study looked at medicine, not walking, so it doesn't prove walking cuts heart risk by the same amount. Still, it shows why a few points can matter.

## How much walking do you need, and how fast?

The walking programs in the Cochrane review averaged about **153 minutes a week**, mostly at moderate intensity, for about 15 weeks [1]. Most took place at home or in the community, not in a lab [1].

That lines up neatly with the CDC's general advice for adults: **150 minutes a week** of moderate activity, such as brisk walking [6]. That's about 30 minutes a day, five days a week. You can also split it into shorter walks if that fits your day better.

### What counts as "brisk"?

You don't need a stopwatch or a heart rate monitor. The CDC lists walking at **2.5 miles per hour or faster** as moderate-intensity activity [7].

An easier check is the talk test. At moderate intensity, you can talk but not sing [7]. If you can belt out a whole chorus, speed up a little. If you can only get out a few words between breaths, you've moved into vigorous territory [7].

### Does a little walking still help?

Yes. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute notes that even a small amount of activity may help, like walking for 10 minutes each day [8]. If 150 minutes sounds like a lot right now, start smaller. Ten minutes is a real starting point, not a failure.

## Why does walking help blood pressure?

When you walk, your heart pumps more blood through your arteries. That extra flow pushes and rubs along the inner lining of your blood vessels [5].

Over time, repeating that stress helps your arteries adapt. Researchers have found that exercise changes blood flow and pressure in ways that can improve how arteries work, and even their size and wall thickness [5]. Those changes play a part in controlling blood pressure in the smaller blood vessels [5].

Put simply, your arteries respond to regular use. Walking gives them steady, gentle practice. Scientists are still working out every detail, but the overall link between activity and lower blood pressure is well established [3].

## Who benefits most from walking?

Pretty much everyone who tried it in the studies saw some benefit.

In the Cochrane review, walking lowered systolic pressure in people 40 and under, people aged 41 to 60, and people over 60 [1]. It helped both women and men [1]. Participants ranged from 16 to 84 years old [1].

A large review done for the U.S. physical activity guidelines found strong evidence for a few key points [3]:

- Being more active is linked to a lower chance of *developing* high blood pressure in the first place.
- Physical activity lowers blood pressure in people with normal blood pressure, slightly raised blood pressure, and high blood pressure.
- People with slightly raised blood pressure (sometimes called prehypertension) tend to see bigger drops than people whose numbers are already normal.

So if your numbers are creeping up, this is a great time to start walking. You may have the most to gain.

## How to start walking for your blood pressure

You don't need special gear or a gym. You need shoes you can walk in and a plan you'll actually stick with. Try this:

1. **Start where you are.** If you're not active now, begin with 10 minutes a day [8]. Add a few minutes each week.
2. **Build toward 150 minutes a week.** Thirty minutes, five days a week, is a simple target [6]. Two 15-minute walks count too.
3. **Pick up the pace.** Aim for a speed where you can talk but not sing [7].
4. **Make it a habit, not an event.** Walk at the same time each day, like after lunch or after dinner. Habits beat motivation.
5. **Give it time.** The trials in the Cochrane review averaged about 15 weeks [1]. Don't judge your progress after one week.
6. **Track it.** A step counter or walking app makes it easy to see your minutes and streaks add up. Seeing progress helps you keep going.
7. **Bring someone along.** A friend, a partner, or a dog can make walking feel less like a chore.

Walking is also generally low-risk. In the Cochrane review, most trials that tracked side effects reported none. A few reported minor issues, most often knee injuries [1]. Good shoes and a gradual build-up help.

## When should you talk to your doctor?

Walking is safe for most people, but check in with your doctor first if any of these apply:

- You've been told you have high blood pressure.
- You have a heart condition, or you've had a heart attack or stroke.
- You take blood pressure medicine.
- You get chest pain, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath when you're active.

The NHLBI advises asking your provider what level of activity is right for you before starting any exercise program [8].

If you take blood pressure medicine, keep taking it as prescribed. Walking works *alongside* medicine, not instead of it. The NHLBI recommends keeping up healthy lifestyle changes while you take blood pressure medicine [8]. If your numbers improve over time, your doctor can decide whether anything about your treatment should change.

It's also worth checking your blood pressure regularly, at home or at your doctor's office. That way you can see whether your walking habit is paying off.

## FAQ

### Is walking enough to lower high blood pressure?

For some people, walking plus other healthy habits may be enough. For others, medicine is still needed. Your doctor can tell you which applies to you [8].

### How long until walking lowers blood pressure?

The trials in the Cochrane review lasted about 15 weeks on average [1]. Give it at least a few months of regular walking before judging results.

### How fast should I walk for blood pressure?

Brisk, not breathless. Most trials used moderate-intensity walking [1]. Aim for a pace where you can talk but not sing [7], for about 150 minutes a week [6].

### Does walking help if my blood pressure is normal?

Yes. Physical activity lowers blood pressure even in people with normal readings, and being active is linked to a lower chance of developing high blood pressure [3].
